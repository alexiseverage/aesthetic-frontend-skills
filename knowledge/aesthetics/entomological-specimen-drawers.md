# Entomological Specimen Drawers

A descriptive material and information-design category centered on preserved insect specimens organized in shallow drawers, boxes and removable unit trays. Repeated comparable bodies, deliberate grouping, mounting depth and retrievable identification form its visual grammar. It is a translation of collection practice, not a named art movement. A decorative butterfly silhouette or a generic cabinet texture alone does not establish it.

## Sources and Context

- [National Museums Scotland, preservation and storage](https://www.nms.ac.uk/our-impact/national-work/training-and-guidance-for-museums/caring-for-entomology-collections/preservation-and-storage-methods) distinguishes dry, slide and fluid preservation; explains direct pinning, micro-pinning, cards and points; and relates taxonomic order to comparison. Its five inspected specimen assemblages support substantial visual variation. Current conservation guidance prefers suitable substrates and sealed storage; old cork and wood in photographs are not construction recommendations.
- [Natural History Museum, entomology collections](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/our-science/services/collections/entomology.html) documents large holdings accumulated over centuries. [Its pinned-insect digitisation workflow](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/our-science/services/collections/digital/programme/pinned-insect-digitisation.html) explains how labels and barcoded identifiers preserve specimen relationships during imaging. Avoid treating digitisation accessories as historic collecting equipment.
- [Mantle, La Salle and Fisher, 2012, whole-drawer imaging](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3406473/) describes high-resolution scanning at CSIRO's Australian National Insect Collection, remote curation and the need to re-image changing drawer contents. Identification from a picture has limits; a polished image is not a universal species-identification tool.
- [National Park Service, curation of insect specimens](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/museums/conserve-o-gram-11-8-curation-of-insect-specimens.htm) contextualizes drawers within trays, cabinets and taxonomic organization. It also distinguishes envelopes and fluid storage, which are neighboring preservation systems rather than the core of this visual category.
- Institutional photographs reproduced through Commons retain creator provenance: [CSIRO 3483](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CSIRO_ScienceImage_3483_Pinned_flies.jpg), [NHM Vorisek drawer 31](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Drawer_31_from_the_Vorisek_collection_of_Cerambycidae_(Natural_History_Museum,_London)_(32134869383).jpg), [Naturalis collection tower](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Naturalis_Biodiversity_Center_-_Museum_-_Collection_tower_06_-_Open_drawer_with_mounted_beetles_include_Dorcadion_suverianum_and_D_morosovi.jpg), and [Bern drawer 4766](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Coleoptera-scarabaeidae-div-4766_(45463862504).jpg). Commons is the hosting/discovery route, not a separate institution or additional artwork.

Historical and functional claims above are source-supported. The linked photographs support observations below. UI behaviors, fonts and tokens are inferred application decisions. Species names in source labels are recorded context, not identifications independently authenticated from the photographs. Collection history includes questions of acquisition, colonial circulation and whose knowledge is represented; this corpus does not establish a single-country origin or a complete global history.

Thus the 25 Commons-hosted qualifying records do not represent 25 independent institutional sources; Bern supplies 13/30 and the sample remains weighted toward European research holdings and beetles. Moths, butterflies, flies, bees, wasps, dragonflies, cockroaches and mixed teaching collections broaden the grammar, without a claim of equal taxonomic representation.

CSIRO 3483 and 3484 show the same unit-tray layout and specimen/label positions from angled/frontal views; one duplicate group, one qualifying artifact. Bytom's two adjacent drawers are grouped as one documented presentation rather than inflated through overlapping views. A drawer's individual specimens, label crops, extracted species images and repeat reproductions count zero additional artifacts. Bern's six modern educational panels and Wellington's wall frame remain useful boundary comparisons but are excluded from the drawer-core count.

NHM's Data Portal presented a security verification page. The Iowa State index provided documented Invertnet links, but the image archive navigation stalled; neither supplied qualifying images. No challenge was solved or access control bypassed. Commons category grids were discovery only. Several Bern archive filenames refer to taxa or drawer portions; no uninspected sibling crop was counted. Reference files were not downloaded or republished, and no generated asset was research evidence. Some Bern files have a Commons/Flickr license review that differs from EXIF copyright metadata; this record makes no republication license inference from that conflict.

Target 30 reached after excluding the panel boundary examples and replacing them with intact drawers. Later batches added diagnostic sketches beside specimens (EB-5048), mint genus labels (EB-4901) and dense horizontal unresolved rows (EB-4892), then confirmed the material/index/comparison relationship. The evidence supports this scoped category; it does not authenticate all labels, endorse collecting methods or establish live insect behavior.

## Analysis

### Claim map and boundary

The non-negotiable relationship is preserved specimen + shallow organizational container + linked identification + comparison across forms or series. The container can be a wood drawer, pale specimen box or nested unit tray, and identifying data can be under the body or adjacent rather than visible as a large front label. EN-03 exposes the layered card/label/pin assembly; EC-3483 and EN-05 show modular groups; EB-5179 and EB-4785 demonstrate taxonomic hierarchy over broad empty fields. A frame of butterfly silhouettes without specimen relationships cannot substitute for this system.

Repetition serves comparison. EV-Vorisek31 documents variation within a named species, while EX-Syria teaches higher groups and EV-Joicey14 arranges large spread butterflies with individual notes. Those are different collection tasks. Do not assume every body is a unique species, every column a different family or every red tag a warning. A source's TYPUS tag is not a generic status badge to reproduce on fictional objects.

Natural diversity prevents a single ornamental silhouette. Metallic shells, fuzzy bodies, delicate wings, cards, pinheads and long antennae each need room. Uniformly scaling bodies into identical tiles removes meaningful relative size, while clipping legs and wings sacrifices the characteristic specimen field. Repeated boxes can be geometric without forcing every group to have the same number of objects. The nearly empty EB-4327 and unresolved EB-4334/4892 contradict a universal dense grid; their missing or provisional status cannot be invented from appearance.

The palette comes largely from specimens, paper and storage materials. NMS green scarabs and Bern metallic beetles are vivid, not an excuse for rainbow UI chrome. EX-Syria's multilingual green cards and EV-Naturalis's yellow pulls/barcodes show contemporary variability. Warm cork, brass pulls and old slips can be quoted selectively, but a uniform sepia filter would hide material distinctions and imply an unsupported antique period.

Seven excluded presentations test the boundary. Bern's modern family panels preserve specimen comparison but show little drawer construction, and the Wellington wall frame teaches order diversity. They can inform readable caption hierarchy while remaining outside this scoped drawer corpus. The Bytom presentation is a more direct drawer variant, including a labeled row-based tray and an adjacent large butterfly tray, grouped once. Evidence of educational display does not justify silently redefining the category as all mounted-insect art.

### Frontend translation (inferred)

A collection catalog explorer can provide a wide drawer-like specimen field, taxon/category filters, a selected-record pane and a working comparison tray. The user should be able to choose records, compare two or three preserved forms, inspect linked labels and clear selections. Original specimen illustrations can carry ridged shells, fuzzy thoraces, transparent wings and pin shadows without copying museum photographs or presenting fictional illustrations as digitized type material.

Use clean light specimen beds, narrow dark rules, restrained wood edge references and one specimen-driven color accent. Group headings, readable scientific names, provenance and IDs should have distinct levels. A contemporary legible serif plus neutral sans or mono identifiers is a proposed typography system, not a claim that all collections use it. Keep functional labels substantially larger than the tiny physical labels shown in photographs. No ornamental Latin, invented coordinates or false accession provenance.

Filter options should reflect actual demo data, update the result count and support an empty state. Selection controls need text labels and visible selected state independent of hue. Comparison should preserve record names, scale caveats and notes, provide remove/clear actions and work by keyboard. Mobile should stack the selected detail and comparison records without horizontally clipping specimen anatomy. Do not require dragging to compare, hover to reveal names or color vision to distinguish status.

Proposed motion should be limited to optional short state transitions. Reduced motion should reveal filtered or selected content immediately, and no specimen should jitter, flap or appear alive in a preserved collection context. Tooltips are optional supplements to persistent labels. Avoid simulating collection handling procedures as an amusement; this is a catalog exploration task, not instruction to collect, kill, trade or manipulate protected insects.
