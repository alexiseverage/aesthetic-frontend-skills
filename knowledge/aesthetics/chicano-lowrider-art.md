### Visual evidence summary
- Denver frames lowriders as both customized vehicles and a way of life: lowered chassis, reupholstered interiors, repainted exteriors, cruising as public display, and cars as "actual works of art." (Denver Art Museum)
- Denver’s exhibition materials also foreground the visual media ecosystem: Lowrider Magazine and related magazines spread lowrider aesthetics globally, and Tony Ortega’s "Check Out the Low Rider, Ese" captures bomba curves, cruising culture, and neighborhood spectatorship. (Denver Art Museum)
- The Smithsonian Sidedoor episode summarizes the historical arc from post-WWII car culture to a symbol of Mexican American pride and self-expression, and explicitly names chrome, paint, defiance, and rolling works of art. (Smithsonian Sidedoor)
- Smithsonian search descriptions for the exhibition and "Inspiration" page emphasize artistic expression, technological innovation, storytelling, and ties to the Chicano movement of the 1960s and 1970s. (Smithsonian search results captured via web_search)

### Seven-dimension synthesis
Palette
- Canonical: candy reds, turquoise/aqua, violet, black, chrome silver, pinstripe white, metallic gold, and high-sun pastel warms. This synthesis is grounded in customized paint, magazine covers, and the exhibition visuals described by Denver and Smithsonian. (Denver Art Museum; Smithsonian Sidedoor)
- Common: mural-like gradients and jewel candy colors contrasted with chrome and whitewall neutrals.
- Variant: monochrome black/chrome elegiac builds or photographic documentary palettes.

Type
- Canonical: hand-painted script, plaque lettering, mural/airbrush title treatments, magazine-cover display faces, and neighborhood-signage adjacency. This comes from the lowrider print ecosystem and Chicano art context, not corporate typography. (Denver Art Museum; Smithsonian search descriptions)
- Avoid flattening it into generic tattoo-blackletter; the lowrider ecosystem is more hybrid, mural, automotive, and magazine-driven.

Texture
- Canonical: chrome, candy paint, clearcoat gloss, velvet upholstery, engraved metal, mural airbrush haze, and sun-baked street surfaces. (Denver Art Museum; Smithsonian Sidedoor)
- Common: reflective lacquer, pinstriping, and tactile upholstery richness.
- Texture should feel crafted and prideful, not rusty "junkyard" by default.

Shape
- Canonical: long low silhouettes, hydraulic stance, Impala curves, script flourishes, mural panels, spoke/rim geometry, and soft automotive contours. (Denver Art Museum; Smithsonian Sidedoor)
- Common: heart, rosary, devotion, and boulevard-cruising iconography in surrounding poster/art contexts, though use with care and context.
- The key shape logic is lowered flow and elongated elegance.

Motion
- Canonical: low-and-slow cruising, bounce/hydraulic lift, procession, and public reveal. Denver explicitly defines the cars through cruising; motion is not optional but constitutive. (Denver Art Museum)
- Digital translation should feel smooth, gliding, proud, and performative rather than fast/aggressive.

Spatial
- Canonical: boulevard display, public gathering, show-and-tell parking, social spectatorship, and neighborhood procession. (Denver Art Museum)
- Common: magazine spread layouts, mural framing, and hero-image composition centered on car-as-portrait.
- Spatially, the car is both object and stage.

- Canonical: Chicana/o identity, postwar customization, cruising, Lowrider Magazine, neighborhood pride, technological ingenuity, and art-as-resistance/self-definition. (Denver Art Museum; Smithsonian Sidedoor; Smithsonian search descriptions)
