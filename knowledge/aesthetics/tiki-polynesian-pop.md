### Visual evidence summary
- Don the Beachcomber’s own summary is useful because it names the classic package plainly: mid-century American faux-Polynesia, bars/restaurants, Chinese food, Caribbean rum cocktails, South Seas décor, exotica music, and postwar backyard/basement diffusion. (Don the Beachcomber)
- Spiers’ history places the origin in 1933 Hollywood and emphasizes movie-set glamour, celebrity culture, theatrical service, portholes, curio cabinets, Chinese-American food, and high-budget immersive interiors designed by people overlapping with film production culture. (How to Eat L.A.)
- SFMOMA’s critique precisely describes the mainland stereotype bundle: bamboo plants, thatch-roof huts, rattan furniture, hanging canoes, floating-band spectacle, and a generalized South Pacific fantasy. (SFMOMA)
- PUNCH provides the contemporary correction: many Pacific and Indigenous voices see tiki as built from appropriation, stereotype, and hospitality spaces that can feel inhospitable to the cultures being sampled. (PUNCH)

### Seven-dimension synthesis
Palette
- Canonical: lagoon turquoise, ocean teal, bamboo/tapa brown, torch amber, hibiscus coral, tropical leaf green, sunset orange, and rum-dark mahogany. (Don the Beachcomber; SFMOMA; How to Eat L.A.)
- Common: black used as night-bar backdrop plus neon aqua/pink in later revival modes.
- Variant: space-age or noir tiki revivals that push the palette darker or more synthetic; use only if deliberately contemporary.

Type
- Canonical: hand-painted sign styles, carved-letter signage, postcard-script accents, menu-board display faces, and mid-century hospitality typography. This is synthesis from restaurant/menu/ephemera culture rather than fine-art lineage. (How to Eat L.A.; Don the Beachcomber)
- Avoid pseudo-tribal fonts and generic "island" novelty faces; they intensify the appropriation problem. (PUNCH; SFMOMA)

Texture
- Canonical: bamboo, rattan, tapa-like patterning, weathered wood, carved mugs, rope, shell, matting, and bar-glow lacquer. (SFMOMA; Don the Beachcomber)
- Common: condensation, ceramic mug gloss, faux-storm ambience, and smoky bar darkness. (How to Eat L.A.)
- Important distinction: texture is central, but should be treated as mid-century hospitality set design, not as an authentic survey of Polynesian material culture. (SFMOMA; PUNCH)

Shape
- Canonical: mug/totem silhouettes, A-frame roofs, outrigger/canoe cues, shield and diamond motifs, boomerang-adjacent mid-century forms, and clustered tabletop garnishes. (Don the Beachcomber; How to Eat L.A.; SFMOMA)
- Common: soft rounded barware, lanterns, mask-like vertical forms, hut geometry.
- Variant: more atomic-age curves when the design leans harder into postwar American kitsch.

Motion
- Canonical digital translation: slow sway, rain/storm ambience, lava-lamp bubbling, torch flicker, and cocktail-theater reveals. This is a direct translation of immersive bar spectacle and exotic escape theatre. (How to Eat L.A.; SFMOMA)
- Avoid cartoon hula loops or mascotized dance motion.

Spatial
- Canonical: immersive environment, low light, enclosure, surprise décor density, and room-as-escape. (How to Eat L.A.; SFMOMA)
- Common: clustered icons, overhead hanging objects, nooks, and layered prop-rich foreground/background.
- The spatial logic is hospitality escapism, not open beach minimalism.

Cultural markers
- Canonical to Polynesian Pop as an American style: faux-South-Pacific scenery, rum cocktails, exotica sound, bar ephemera, celebrity-era Hollywood fantasy, backyard-luau spread. (Don the Beachcomber; How to Eat L.A.)
- Canonical to current critical framing: appropriation discourse, Pasifika critique, and the need to separate Pacific peoples from the fantasy machine built around them. (PUNCH; SFMOMA)

### Related-aesthetic boundaries
- Vs. mid-century modern: tiki/Polynesian Pop is a subset/adjacent branch of mid-century leisure culture with more props, nightlife, and tropical escapism; MCM proper is broader, cleaner, and more materially disciplined. (How to Eat L.A.; Don the Beachcomber)
- Vs. atomic-age/googie: those emphasize futurist optimism and roadside architecture; tiki emphasizes South-Pacific fantasy and immersive bar culture.
