# Plakatstil

Plakatstil is the early twentieth-century German poster-design direction associated with graphic simplification, forceful advertising hierarchy, and makers including Lucian Bernhard, Ludwig Hohlwein, and Hans Rudi Erdt. The narrower **Sachplakat**, or object-poster, branch isolates a recognizable product and its name. The wider corpus also includes figures, event posters, scenes, and political messages. These related terms should share one catalog owner while the object-poster branch retains explicit subset guidance.

## Source / Evidence Links

- [Neue Galerie, The Modern Poster in Germany](https://www.neuegalerie.org/exhibitions/modern-poster-germany) — **source-supported** historical framing, Bernhard's role, and the reaction against ornate Art Nouveau poster design.
- [Kunstbibliothek, Early Posters, 1840–1914](https://www.smb.museum/en/museums-institutions/kunstbibliothek/collection-research/research/early-posters/) — **source-supported** printing and collection context; identifies Hollerbaum & Schmidt and multiple poster designers. Its collection spans several styles, so collection membership alone does not classify a poster.
- [Smithsonian, Lucian Bernhard and REM advertising](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/emergence-modernism-early-20th-century-american-advertising-lucian-bernhard-and-his) — **source-supported** distinction between pared-down object posters and more figurative Plakatstil advertising, including later American applications.
- [LACMA, Julius Paul Poster Collection](https://unframed.lacma.org/2024/05/09/julius-paul-poster-collection) — **source-supported** street-display context, simplified mass communication, the collection's stylistic breadth, and its restitution history.
- Individual MoMA and MK&G object pages below provide **source-supported** title, attribution, medium, and date. The linked reproductions provide **directly observed** formal evidence.

These historical/context pages were read. They are not themselves extra visual artifacts. No source artwork is reproduced in this profile or licensed for inclusion in a new interface by this research.

## Dimension Synthesis

“Consistent” and “common” are qualitative conclusions from this corpus, not percentages or claims about every historical poster. Color descriptions are estimated from visual inspection; no exact historical pigment or sampled CSS color is asserted.

| Dimension | Consistent / defining | Common | Variant / boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Palette** | Strong figure/ground distinction and a limited set of dominant color roles | Black or deep ground with pale type; orange, red, blue, green, or ochre emphasis | Pale commercial fields and shaded fabrics also occur; primary-color-only palettes are not required |
| **Type** | Emphatic brand, venue, product, or message hierarchy tied to the composition | Large painted capitals, weight contrast, compact subordinate copy | Serif, sans, script, italic, and blackletter examples occur; one mandatory type family would misrepresent the corpus |
| **Texture** | Graphic reproduction rather than photographic interface chrome | Lithographic color planes, drawn contours, subordinate tonal modeling | Existing paper wear is not the original style's necessary intent; Cords uses shaded cloth; fine grain cannot be inferred from the smaller scans |
| **Shape** | Recognizable subject reduced to readable silhouettes or large planes | Enlarged products, cropped figures, strong overlaps, diagonal presentation | Figurative, floral, patterned, technical, and landscape variants; a rigid circles-and-triangles vocabulary belongs elsewhere |
| **Motion** | Historical motion behavior is **unknown**: all qualifying evidence is still media | Implied direction through poses, arrows, diagonals, and repeated forms | UI transitions are **inferred application choices**, not measured historical behavior |
| **Spatial** | Subject/message dominance with a clear compositional hierarchy | One focal object plus name; large image zone and anchored lettering zone | Multiple objects, smaller copy, borders, and more narrative arrangements occur in the wider style |
| **Cultural markers** | Commercial and public communication in the German-speaking early modern poster context | Named products, retailers, venues, lithographic printers, consumer goods, entertainment | Wartime and political applications are documented variants; later American Bernhard work is a continuation, not evidence that every modern ad is Plakatstil |

## Analysis

### Classification and origin

**Source-supported:** museum histories describe a shift from ornate late nineteenth-century poster art toward simplified, forceful advertising. The named commercial makers and printing context distinguish the term from a newly invented internet label. **Directly observed:** the corpus repeatedly ties recognizable subjects and dominant lettering into a graphic hierarchy. **Decision:** a new canonical aesthetic is justified alongside Art Nouveau, Bauhaus, and Polish Poster School; it is not an alias for any of them.

The name Sachplakat is handled as a scoped subset/alias within the Plakatstil owner. A user asking for the object-poster version should receive the single-product and short-name composition. Wider Plakatstil may use people and scenes. It would be inaccurate to require every example to contain just one isolated item, no border, no modeling, and a heavy sans font.

### Observed grammar and meaningful exceptions

R02–R04, R06, R08–R09, and R26 establish product visibility, simplified planes, and dramatic brand hierarchy. R05's detailed cloth folds challenge an absolutely flat-rendering rule. R01, R07, R10–R11, and R29 challenge a sans-only rule. R12–R21 and R22–R28 expand the system into service, event, clothing, and figurative advertising. They retain hierarchy and graphic simplification while varying density and subject count.

R10–R11 and R22 document political uses. Their ideological text is contextual evidence, not a required design motif. R29's Manoli and R03's Manoli advertise the same brand but are different designs by different makers; shared brand name is not duplication. R30 establishes a later figurative commercial application, without expanding the boundary to all modern advertising.

### Proposed frontend translation

Use a product/venue as the focal graphic and let the main name occupy a deliberate compositional zone. Keep supporting descriptions, price, selection, and purchase or inquiry controls semantically separate and readable. The object-poster branch works well as an interactive small catalog: selecting a model changes the dominant original illustration and the real details beneath it.

A typewriter catalog is a promising showcase: an original vector machine illustration, emphatic display name, limited field colors, and three genuine selectable models. Product selection can swap graphic planes immediately or use a brief optional reveal; historical timing is unknown, so any duration is a proposed UI value. A reduced-motion setting should use an immediate state change. Accessible focus and readable support text must survive at narrow widths.

No fixed CSS colors or fonts are extracted from these images. Proposed tokens should identify ink, paper, subject fill, and one accent; use categories such as broad serif display, painted block lettering, or compact sans according to the chosen subset. The source variation argues for clear roles rather than an obligatory font brand.

### Rights, connotation, and cultural framing

The intended mode is **contemporary revival**, grounded in the commercial subset rather than literal historical brand reproduction. Keep original companies, protected marks, caricatures, and political slogans in analysis links. New assets should be newly drawn and use their own brand/content. The LACMA provenance account records dispossession and restitution of a historical poster collection; that history should not be erased when describing the material as decorative nostalgia.

Source-supported printer and museum labels do not make all digitized posters members of this style. Other Art Nouveau, Socialist Realist, or later Art Deco objects in the same collections are neighbors or counterexamples unless separately supported. Do not classify through museum membership alone.

## Connections

- **`art-nouveau`** — same-period predecessor/neighbor. Plakatstil's principal hierarchy and graphic reduction differ from Art Nouveau's continuous organic structural ornament. R01 and R19 retain ornament or floral subject matter without requiring Art Nouveau's total-surface line system.
- **`bauhaus`** — shares simplification, but commercial subject recognition and expressive lettering replace Bauhaus's workshop-derived formal construction as the organizing premise.
- **`polish-poster-school`** — shares poster communication and authored imagery; Plakatstil commonly prioritizes immediately legible product/venue recognition where the Polish school may favor interpretive metaphor.
- **`wartime-propaganda`** — a documented subject application in R10–R11/R22, not a synonym or obligatory core.
- **`wpa-poster-style`** — shares economical printed color and bold forms, but belongs to a different American public-program context and often organizes landscape/civic scenes rather than this German commercial lineage.
