# Pichwai

Pichwai is an established tradition of devotional hangings associated with the Hindu Pushtimarg community and Krishna as Shrinathji. Framed figures, meaningful seasonal and festival motifs, repeated registers, decorative textile surfaces and deliberate scale hierarchy structure the visual system. Painted cotton dominates this corpus, with source-described embroidered variants. An external three-dimensional icon can supply the devotional center, so the hanging need not depict a large Krishna itself.

**Evidence status:** thirty-one useful complete works meet the ten-work floor and thirty-work target. The local canonical pair follows maintainer self-review; publication and the paired website release remain pending. No historical interface, font, exact hex palette or animation timing has been recovered. Analytical reference images are not production assets.

## Source / Evidence Links

- [Met shrine hanging record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/78209): contextual text identifies a backdrop for Krishna, Nathdwara and Shrinathji. Its unavailable photograph contributes no visual count.
- [Smithsonian: Delighting Krishna](https://asia.si.edu/whats-on/exhibitions/delighting-krishna/): eighteenth- to twentieth-century cloth hangings, community interpretation and multisensory devotional display. Court paintings shown alongside them are not automatically Pichwai.
- [Smithsonian interactive](https://asia.si.edu/interactives/pichwai-portals/index.html): source-selected works and community/curator/conservation accounts explain Braj, external icons and Nathdwara/Deccan contexts.
- [TAPI Pichhwais collection](https://tapicollection.org/category/collections/indian-textiles/pichhwais-religious-textiles/): painted, embroidered, woven and printed production routes, plus other religious textiles that are not automatically Pichhwai.
- [MAP: Deccan Pichwai](https://map-india.org/visual-storage/drawer-25/): Gujarati merchant patronage, local Deccan processes and domestic/temple display. Its fragment photographs were not inspected or counted here.
- [Smithsonian pigment study](https://asia.si.edu/whats-on/blog/posts/identifying-the-pichwai-painters-palette/): a scientific investigation of fourteen paintings documents changing pigment choices, including imported synthetic colors. It disproves a universal natural-pigment rule; it does not supply UI color tokens or recipes for all Pichwai.

## Direct Media Audit

The ledger has **34 records**, **32 complete-media inspections** and **31 qualifying unique still works**. One Met conservation detail is a duplicate of its complete object sheet; two unavailable records remain excluded. No thumbnail survey, historical video, website interaction or generated asset contributes to the count. Five museum-offered analytical originals were downloaded to resolve soft or cropped initial viewer frames; their hashes and dimensions were verified. The rest are linked complete native images or actual full museum viewers. No source media are proposed for production reuse.

Three independently owning useful collections contribute: Met one, Smithsonian twenty-four and TAPI six. Smithsonian's 24/31 concentration and its strong Nathdwara emphasis are explicit limitations, not a population survey. TAPI and Met add Deccan, Kishangarh, Gujarat and material variants. Maker names are absent from the consulted records; this does not establish anonymity within the makers' communities. The corpus favors historical devotional textiles and does not describe every current secular adaptation or living workshop.

Exclusions, corrections and access limits:

- Met 2012.487.4 and British Museum 1959,0411,0.10 explicitly have no available image. The latter is described as painted paper; it is a terminology/medium lead, not visual evidence.
- The Met sheet contains complete front and reverse photographs of one object, not two works. Native inspection corrected an initial six-figure estimate to eight; the dated correction remains in the ledger.
- Shared festival titles, raised-arm icons or cow/lotus motifs are not duplicate evidence by themselves. The Met/Smithsonian Deccan works, full-moon variants and two 1870–1880 Annakut paintings were compared at the whole-composition level.
- Two TAPI pages have conflicting accession labels and linked image filenames. Their separately titled complete images are clearly distinct and source-declared Pichhwai variants, but exact accession, date and detailed material attribution remain provisional. They do not establish origin dates or microscopic embroidery techniques.
- Smithsonian S1992.26's height/width labels conflict with the complete landscape image aspect; the apparent inversion remains unresolved. S1992.70's festival identification explicitly says “possibly.”
- Main-page thumbnails with no enlargement control contributed zero. Where offered, the institution's actual IIIF/Mirador link revealed complete useful images. Missing or soft tiles were rechecked; cropped viewer frames were resolved by fitting the whole image or inspecting an explicitly offered original. No guessed scale URL or access restriction bypass was used.
- Museum frames, mounts, conservation tears and material loss are presentation/condition, not required decorative motifs. Rights notices remain attached to analytical references.

## Analysis

### Claim-to-artifact map

The devotional setting and patterned hierarchy recur across the three collections. Central icon/attendant structures occur in works 2, 7–8, 10–11, 14, 18–22 and 25–30. External-icon or nonfigural branches occur in 1, 4–5, 9, 12 and 16. Circular or repeated narrative arrangements occur in 3, 6, 15, 17 and 27; architecture becomes dominant in 10, 22 and 24, and water becomes dominant in 31. This supports a tradition organized around devotional display, rather than one compulsory picture template.

Repeat is purposeful: shrine panels frame a center, cows create borders or a field, peacock tails provide visual scale, and narrative groups organize episodes. Works 13, 28–30 reinforce dense ritual framing without proving that every small unit has the same meaning. Works 22 and 24 allow more spacious architecture, and 31 allows a broad pale field. Dense ornament is common, but clutter or maximum saturation is not mandatory.

The type evidence is limited but adequate for an optional caption branch. Works 14, 17 and 25 show integrated textual marks; no identified historic digital font follows. Do not fill this gap with invented Devanagari-like lettering or use a novelty “Indian” font as a cultural shortcut. Contemporary readable typography is an explicit proposal.

### Material, history and interpretation

Primary sources explain why the hanging is behind an icon and how season, festival, adornment and viewing relate to display. That devotional relationship distinguishes Pichwai from general floral textile luxury. Source-described embroidery broadens the material range, while the exact TAPI accession conflicts remain bounded. Scientific analysis documents changing and imported pigments in fourteen Smithsonian works; neither all-natural pigments nor a timeless universal color recipe is defensible.

Keep Nathdwara, Deccan and other source places distinct. A large framed image or lotus alone does not identify every Indian painting as Pichwai. Nathdwara painting includes broader genres; the British Museum paper record is an uninspected terminology lead, not a reason to silently classify all paper miniatures. Do not infer each gopi, priest, food, gesture or sacred symbol from appearance when the record does not explain it.

The sample is institutionally concentrated and historical. It cannot certify living-practitioner consensus, current devotional schedules or secular home-decor usage. Unknown makers are not proof of a universal anonymous-workshop system. Displaying a source image, creating a modern study and installing a ritual hanging are different acts; the website must state its actual role and attribution.

### Interface proposal and stopping decision

A contemporary gallery reading guide can present original seasonal hanging studies with an accordion for their contexts and a focused detail panel explaining selected motifs. Artwork should preserve a devotional focal relationship, border/register hierarchy and meaningful motif scale. Plain controls and text outside dense fields keep interpretation readable. Choosing a study should change both its actual artwork and the accompanying contextual text; a decorative theme switch alone is insufficient.

Tokens, font choices, breakpoints and modal behavior belong to the application handoff, not historical evidence. Intentional stillness suits the researched still corpus. An educational original depiction can acknowledge Pushtimarg context without pretending to be an authenticated or consecrated historical object, copying a museum work or presenting sacred images as a generic brand mascot.

Collection reached thirty useful works, then added Jal Vihar because a primary scientific source pointed to a materially different water/palette branch. The final comparison strengthened breadth rather than padding a number. The remaining gaps concern exact lettering, provisional catalog metadata, microscopic process and living-practitioner coverage; they do not supply invented defining rules. Local authoring retains those caveats after maintainer self-review.

## Connections

- `mingei`: both offer process and textile/craft vocabulary, but Mingei's beauty-of-use and collecting framework differs from Pichwai's specific Pushtimarg devotional backdrop and festival narrative.
- `maximalism`: patterned abundance can overlap, but density without the devotional frame, register meaning and focal relationship is insufficient for Pichwai.
- `art-nouveau`: flowers, peacocks and flowing contours superficially overlap; Belle Époque total-design/whiplash logic is a different history and composition system. No direct influence is asserted here.
- `dia-de-los-muertos-calavera-graphics`: a comparison of meaningful devotional/memorial display, not a shared culture or interchangeable motif vocabulary. Mexican remembrance and Pushtimarg devotion remain distinct.
- Nathdwara painting, general Mewar painting, all Hindu religious art, temple architecture and every Indian floral textile are not aliases.
- Monsoon/Deccan gilding, autumn full moon, circular dance, cow-festival field, Annakut display, architecture and lotus-water studies are prose branches, not invented additional canonical slugs.
