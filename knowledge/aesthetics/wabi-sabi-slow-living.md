## Analysis

Wabi-Sabi / Slow Living is application-ready only when its signal is distributed across all seven dimensions rather than resting on palette alone. The load-bearing pattern is: imperfection + impermanence + humble materiality + contemplative spatial restraint. Frontend translation should begin with that pattern, then apply color, texture, shape, and motion as evidence of it.

Do not flatten into beige wellness minimalism or fake Zen branding; keep tea culture, impermanence, humble materiality, and aging visible.

### Visual evidence summary
- The Met explains wabi through tea culture: beauty found in rustic Korean and Japanese vessels, unrefined or imperfect forms, natural ash glazes, asymmetry, rough clay surfaces, and connoisseurial appreciation of humble objects. (Met)
- The Met’s examples of Shigaraki, Bizen, Raku, Shino, and Oribe establish the tactile vocabulary: fiery orange clay, speckled feldspar bumps, deep reddish-brown surfaces, hand-molded bowls, and kiln-produced unpredictability. (Met)
- Britannica’s Sen Rikyū article grounds the historical pivot: deliberate simplicity, appreciation of the old and faded, smaller and more secluded tea houses, rustic tea bowls, and wabi/sabi as formal ideals rather than just mood words. (Britannica, Sen Rikyū)
- Musubi is not a primary authority, but it is useful for contemporary translation into everyday objects, care, patina, and the way modern lifestyle discourse turns wabi-sabi into a domestic ethic. Use cautiously and label it contemporary interpretation. (Musubi Kiln)

### Seven-dimension synthesis
Palette
- Canonical: clay white, ash gray, soot black, moss/lichen green, weathered brown, muted earth red, and the subdued off-whites of paper, plaster, and linen. (Met; Britannica, Sen Rikyū)
- Common: soft plant greens, tea green, pale wood, smoke, and stone.
- Variant: warmer beige/greige wellness palettes in contemporary "slow living" adaptations; these are adjacent, not the historical core. (Musubi Kiln as contemporary reading)

Type
- Canonical translation for digital work: restrained serif or humanist sans with low contrast in hierarchy, modest scale shifts, and plenty of breathing room. The historical system is object/space/ritual driven rather than typographic, so typography should communicate quietness, age, and non-showiness rather than literal Japanese stylization. (Met; Britannica)
- Avoid brush-font cliché, Zen quote aesthetics, or fake shodō pastiche.

Texture
- Canonical: unglazed or irregular stoneware, natural ash glaze, rough clay, patina, worn wood, bamboo, handmade paper, and visible repair or aging. (Met; Britannica, wabi-sabi; Musubi Kiln)
- Common: matte textiles, linen, softened edges, weathering, and subtle stain/age traces.
- Texture should feel touched by time rather than distressed for effect.

Shape
- Canonical: asymmetrical bowls, slightly off-center vessels, modest hut-like enclosure, low tables, natural-edge objects, and forms shaped by hand or kiln contingency. (Met; Britannica, Sen Rikyū)
- Common: rounded but irregular silhouettes, small openings, compressed proportions, and humble utilitarian geometry.
- Avoid perfect circles, luxury polish, or overdesigned sculptural drama.

Motion
- Canonical digital motion: minimal, slow, almost imperceptible. Good cues are steam drift, breathing fades, and gentle reveal-by-presence. This is synthesis from chanoyu ritual and the emphasis on contemplation. (Met; Britannica)
- Avoid delight animations or high-friction transitions.

Spatial
- Canonical: secluded, sparse, intimate, and deliberately scaled down; Britannica notes Sen Rikyū’s move toward smaller tea spaces, while the Met emphasizes withdrawal from the mundane world in a hut-like room. (Britannica, Sen Rikyū; Met)
- Common: negative space that feels contemplative rather than luxury-empty; placement matters more than quantity.
- Variant: modern slow-living interiors may enlarge the scale, but if too airy/polished they drift into Scandinavian or warm minimalism instead. (Musubi Kiln; compare repo warm-minimalism/scandinavian-modern entries)

Cultural markers
- Canonical: chanoyu, rustic tea bowls, natural imperfection, weathering, age, connoisseurship of humble objects, and the ideals of wabi and sabi. (Met; Britannica)
- Contemporary adjacent markers: slow routines, care for objects over time, repair, handmade domesticity, anti-perfection stance. (Musubi Kiln)
