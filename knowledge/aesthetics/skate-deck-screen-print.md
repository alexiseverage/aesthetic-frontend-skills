## Analysis

Skate deck screen-print graphics are best understood as a print-process system, not a subject-matter genre. The defining constraint is the screen-print method itself: each color is a separate screen, ink is mixed to match a spot color, and the graphic is built from a small number of flat, separated inks rather than continuous tone. This produces the aesthetic's signature look — flat saturated color, visible halftone dots where gradients are faked, and the wood grain of the deck showing through the ink.

The composition grammar is the deck silhouette. Graphics are designed to fill a popsicle or shaped deck, with the brand logotype often functioning as the graphic itself (1970s logo decks) before illustration took over in the 1980s under artists like Jim Phillips (Santa Cruz) and Vernon Courtlandt Johnson (Powell Peralta). The iconography draws on punk/skate-punk culture — skulls, monsters, hot-rod and surf motifs — with a direct lineage to Ed Roth and 1960s psychedelic rock poster art.

For frontend translation, the useful rules are: a limited spot-color palette (2–6 inks), flat fills with halftone texture for any gradient, a strong single dominant graphic, and a silhouette-shaped composition frame. The result should read as ink on a physical surface, not as a clean vector illustration.
