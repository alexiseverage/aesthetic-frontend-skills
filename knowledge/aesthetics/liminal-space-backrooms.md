## Analysis

Vacancy is relational: check-in counters, waiting chairs, sports courts and corridors imply the people who normally use them. Repeated fixtures and blocked sightlines keep routes unresolved. The original Backrooms photograph adds low ceiling, carpet, patterned wallpaper and a yellow cast; the wider corpus shows that none of those particular materials or hues is universal.

Claim-to-artifact map: BR-DSC00161 grounds the yellow-room branch; JF-airport_stop_go_1600 and 75-On-the-Outside-Looking-In ground expected-use absence; JF-jn_paris_markthalle_1200_LUMF, 75-Pedway and FM-Kotopoulis ground repeated perspective; FM-Kalantaridou and FM-Kallias ground framed access; FM-Stavrakara and JF-LOST_grancanaria_112017-2_1600 ground idle leisure. FM-Papalexandrou and FM-Chorianopoulou show strong color variants. Landscape, art-object and facade-only exclusions keep generic melancholy from swallowing the boundary.

### Source bibliography / URLs

- [LS1] explains liminal-space TikTok as empty, transitional, nostalgic, eerie montage culture and explicitly links hashtag adjacency with dreamcore and weirdcore.
- [LS2] frames liminal spaces as emotional architecture: corridors, waiting spaces, unoccupied transition zones, nostalgia, uncertainty.
- [LS3] gives the strongest art-theory framing of Backrooms as post-capitalist, uncanny, repetitive architecture and links noclipping to glitch/system error.
- [LS4] documents how the Backrooms’ fluorescent, yellow, carpeted maze became a mainstream production design reference and quotes the “ordinary but wrong” effect.
- [LS5] supplies the basic chronology: 2019 4chan creepypasta post, 2002 source photo, 2022 Kane Parsons web-series expansion.

### Visual evidence summary

- Empty corridors, abandoned shopping centres, barren parking lots, old party footage, and depopulated everyday interiors dominate liminal-space imagery [LS1][LS2].
- The Backrooms branch crystallizes around mono-yellow rooms, fluorescent hum, damp carpet, repetitive office-like segmentation, and infinite maze logic [LS3][LS4][LS5].
- The shared affect is familiarity stripped of normal use: spaces are mundane, but human absence and repetitive geometry make them uncanny [LS2][LS3][LS4].
- Backrooms intensifies liminal space by adding extradimensional horror and looped entrapment rather than remaining purely atmospheric or nostalgic [LS1][LS3][LS5].

### Seven-dimension synthesis

- Palette: sickly fluorescent yellow, nicotine beige, off-white ceiling tile, institutional gray, faded carpet brown, sodium-lit parking-lot darkness; occasional pool-tile aqua in related sub-variants, but the canonical Backrooms branch is yellowed and stale [LS1][LS3][LS4][LS5].
- Type: utilitarian signage fonts, low-information wayfinding text, timestamp overlays, VHS captions, or no typography at all; when text appears, it should feel municipal, archival, or system-generated rather than expressive [LS1][LS2][LS4].
- Texture: low-resolution compression, VHS fuzz, fluorescent glare, stained carpet, acoustic ceiling tile, drywall, emptiness, soft image blur, and ambient grain [LS1][LS2][LS4].
- Shape: rectilinear hallways, dropped ceilings, doorframes, repetitive office partitions, mall atriums, stairwells, pool edges, and endless right-angle segmentation [LS2][LS3][LS4].
- Motion: slow drift, stillness, looping camera walk, analog zoom, found-footage shake, or almost no motion; suspense comes from delay and repetition rather than action [LS1][LS4].
- Spatial: threshold environments, transitional architecture, infinite or unresolved circulation, no clear destination, no crowd, no anchor object, and a constant sense of being between identities or uses [LS1][LS2][LS3].
- Cultural markers: noclipping, creepypasta lore, fluorescent hum, early-2000s childhood locations, dead malls, empty schools, analog horror, and post-pandemic nostalgia for depopulated shared space [LS1][LS3][LS4][LS5].

### Related-aesthetic boundaries

- Distinct from `hauntology`: hauntology is media-memory and cultural residue; liminal-space/backrooms is more architectural and spatially depopulated, with less emphasis on obsolete media formats as primary signifier [LS1][LS3].
- Distinct from `dreamcore-weirdcore`: overlap exists, but liminal-space/backrooms stays closer to plausible built environments and transitional space; dreamcore/weirdcore introduce more surreal editing, text, or dream logic [LS1][DW1][DW4].
- Distinct from `gothic-revival` or horror-goth styles: fear comes from banality, vacancy, and repetition, not ornament, decay romanticism, or historical darkness [LS3][LS4].

### Implementation notes for dictionary entries

- Non-negotiables: transitional architecture + human absence + unresolved repetition + uncanny familiarity.
- Treat “Liminal Space” and “Backrooms” as linked but non-identical: the former is broader and can be wistful; the latter is fictionalized extradimensional horror descended from the 2019 creepypasta [LS1][LS3][LS5].
- Include a note that this aesthetic frequently uses low-fi capture and analog degradation to intensify memory and unreality, but pristine photography can still read correctly if the spatial composition is strong enough [LS1][LS2].
- Good related entries: `hauntology`, `dreamcore-weirdcore`, `analog-horror` if that family is later added.
