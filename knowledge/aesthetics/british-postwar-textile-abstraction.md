# British Postwar Textile Abstraction

A scoped material/design vocabulary focused on abstract and substantially abstracted furnishing prints of the 1950s, with explicitly identified formative 1940s woven examples. This is a useful umbrella, not a claim that eight designers belonged to one self-named movement. Nonobjective geometry, simplified organic forms and some macro-botanical abstraction coexist. Conventional pictorial bouquets are outside this scope.

## Source / Evidence Links

- [V&A: Post-war textiles](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/post-war-textiles) places restrained wartime production, the 1951 Festival of Britain and subsequent abstract furnishing design in context. It identifies émigré designers, different manufacturers and Althea McNish’s Trinidad background. The collection is not evidence for an exclusively white, native-born or single-designer British style.
- [V&A: Lucienne Day](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/lucienne-day-an-introduction) contextualizes printed pattern and industrial manufacture. Individual object records below distinguish design and manufacture dates and several different fibers/processes.
- [National Archives: Mahler and Groag](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/textile-designs-by-marian-mahler-and-jacqueline-groag/) supplies actual Board of Trade registration photographs and registered design numbers, with David Whitehead production and émigré context. A shared BT volume can contain several distinct designs. A registration photograph is not necessarily a color-accurate cloth reproduction.
- [MoMA: British Printed Textiles](https://www.moma.org/calendar/exhibitions/3685) contextualizes industrial printing, domestic/corporate use and collaboration. Its 1960s works and installation photographs are not counted as additional 1950s designs.
- MoMA object records: [Magnetic](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/196748), [Mezzanine](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/196749), [Bark Cloth](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/196753). These support roller-printed cotton, screen-printed cotton crepe and screen-printed cotton respectively; they do not establish one universal material.

### B21 — Tropic, 1959

Vivid pink/violet field is crossed by loose large red/pink blossoms and green/dark leaves with pale drawn veins, occasional turquoise slivers and broad scratchy/dragged-looking pigment marks. Organic forms remain recognizable but their overlapping brushlike patches, non-naturalistic color and irregular boundaries make the gesture/cloth surface dominant. ## Analysis

The stable system is the relationship between fine connectors, irregular bounded masses, repeat intervals and a legible material ground. It includes airy and dense, geometric and painterly, monochrome and saturated variants. A sample configurator can translate those relationships into uneven section identifiers, thin ruled labels, repeat-scale choices and a useful sample set. It should not claim that real museum designs used interactive controls or reproduce a famous Calyx motif. Proposed color values, type stacks and control dimensions are new application decisions.

## Connections

Mid-century Modern is the broader period context; Scandinavian Modern is a comparison in furnishing design, not the same national/industrial history. Atomic Age overlaps some geometry, but rockets and starbursts are unnecessary. UPA Graphic Modernism shares selective line/fill and open fields, but its scene/pose staging differs from textile repeat and sample-book organization. No independent subset slug is asserted.
