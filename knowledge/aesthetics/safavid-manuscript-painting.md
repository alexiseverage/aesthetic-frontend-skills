# Safavid Manuscript Painting

## Scope and Sources

A separately useful narrative manuscript-painting system within Persian art, bounded here to sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Safavid production. It is not an independent modern movement, all Persian painting, all Islamic ornament, or all Safavid drawings. The inspected corpus includes epic, historical and literary scenes, not only mythical beings. Cultural setting of a story does not establish place of production: Walters W.649 depicts an Indian story in a Safavid Iranian manuscript.

Three independently useful museum contexts support the classification. The [Met historical essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-safavids-before-1600) explains court workshop continuities and manuscript patronage, then the increasing prominence of single-page painting. [Cleveland's White Div record](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1988.96) identifies a Tahmasp manuscript example and qualified makers. [Walters W.648](https://thedigitalwalters.org/Data/WaltersManuscripts/html/W648/description.html) and [W.649](https://thedigitalwalters.org/Data/WaltersManuscripts/html/W649/description.html) distinguish painters, scribes, literary texts, illustration sequences and later bindings. The [Met Concourse record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/451725) identifies a later painting within a manuscript containing earlier Timurid work. That mixed chronology prevents blanket dating from the volume's title.

Dates, titles and attributions below follow object records, not deductions from pictures. In particular, an API's author field is not a painter attribution. Institutional attributions may change; qualifiers are retained. Current public main was checked against `678a234444fc8452b1945346c2f47ae3a0576e22` during this continuation.

## Direct Media Audit

Thirty full-media inspections; zero qualifying videos, websites or generated evidence; zero unresolved identities and zero duplicate versions retained. Met 11, Cleveland 9, Walters 10. All 30 reference images were downloaded as permitted private analytical copies with exact URLs, native dimensions and SHA-256 hashes in the private ledger. No historical image is included in the website assets. Met images are explicitly public domain; Cleveland images CC0. Walters image listings state CC BY 3.0 while manuscript descriptions separately state CC BY-NC-SA 3.0; retain attribution, keep copies private for analysis, and do not treat these pages as blanket commercial clearance.

Whole Met originals range approximately 2500–2800 by 3700–4000 pixels; Cleveland print JPEGs are 3400 pixels high; Walters' explicitly labeled large JPEGs are 1800 pixels high, distinct from its thumbnails. Each was viewed individually at a useful full-composition scale. The legible marks support composition, contour and local pattern observations, not pigment analysis, transcription or microscopic conservation claims. Native scan dimensions are recorded separately from display scaling.

Counting unit: independently composed narrative painting, not book, page screenshot, crop or scene fragment. Eleven paintings belong to the Tahmasp Shahnama (ten Met and one Cleveland), eight to W.649 by one painter, and two to W.648. Thus 30 paintings do not mean 30 independent workshops or manuscripts. Cleveland parent/recto/verso duplicates and text-only sides were screened out before acquisition. One full painting is counted even when its page contains multiple narrative groups. This is concentrated surviving museum evidence, especially elite books; it is not a demographic survey of all Safavid production.

Discovery used documented museum APIs and archive catalogs: the Met search returned 93 metadata leads and Cleveland 97 mixed results, with duplicates, other media and non-Safavid works excluded. Prior network timeouts were revalidated and then recovered through public documented endpoints. British Museum 1948,1211,0.23 had an unavailable viewer; AKM156 remained a thumbnail/access lead; Met 453311 explicitly disallowed image enlargement/download and was left as contextual text. No bypass or forced download was used. Target 30 and floor 10 both met without a scarcity exception.

Later batches added pale Shiraz architecture, relatively sparse figures, no-text illustrated sides, two-column seventeenth-century pages, a dark narrative flame and blue outer margins. These change the application guidance materially: neither four columns, gold speckling, dark blue, dense crowds nor border-breaking is universal. The final additions reinforce the layered reading hierarchy while leaving regional attribution and precise materials uncertain. Stop at 30 because representative compositional variants and three collections are covered, not because the schema's ten-item threshold alone passed.

## Analysis

The useful boundary is a narrative page system, not a single ornament or subject. Layered landscape and architecture establish scene relationships; fine local patterns differentiate cloth, plants, rocks and surfaces; ruled or interlocking text/picture compartments support sequential looking. Manuscript pages are culturally and materially specific even when a modern application uses English text and original fiction. Shared cream paper does not merge this with English Bestiary or Insular Gospel ornament. Edo woodblock shares narrative print organization but differs in medium, historical lineage and graphic treatment.

Non-negotiables for a contemporary quotation are the composed narrative reading surface, purposeful layered pictorial space, fine local detail against quieter areas, and honest attribution to the bounded tradition. Common but not required: gold, small figures, cream calligraphy panels, blue and violet, thin rectangular rules. Variants and counterexamples above reject mandatory crowds, four columns, dark grounds and ornamental margins. S18's published painting has no visible page border; do not infer the appearance of a lost or unseen surrounding leaf.

Software transfer is a design inference. A manually navigated picture story can pair a large illustration with a heading, short prose panels and persistent chapter controls. A visible completion state, concise live announcements, full image framing, keyboard controls and single-column mobile prose test whether the system works as software. None is claimed to be a historical behavior. The planned fictional garden uses original generated art and newly written prose, explicitly not translated Persian poetry, religious allegory or reconstructed folklore. Generated imagery is excluded from research counts.
