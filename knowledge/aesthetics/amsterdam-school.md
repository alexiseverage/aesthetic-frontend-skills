# Amsterdam School

## Scope and Classification

A distinct historical-design-movements entry for Dutch expressive architecture and applied design, centered on approximately 1910–1930. The visual system connects purposeful volumes, material articulation, crafted detail and an integrated hierarchy across building, furniture, surface and lettering. It is not limited to red brick in Amsterdam. Inspected examples also come from The Hague, Bergen, Groningen and Bergen op Zoom, and include wood, metal, textile, ceramics and graphic work.

Wendingen supplies an associated editorial strand, not an alias for the whole movement or a guarantee that every contributor's art belongs to it. Count the inspected editor's typography/framing, not every photographed chair or dancer on a spread. Likewise, a designer's membership does not establish every later work's classification. Cohen's 1930 vacuum folder and 1933 Halewijn reproduction remain adjacent graphic boundaries; the 1933 greeting card is a bounded later continuation, not evidence that the movement's architectural center moved to the 1930s.

## Analysis

### Identity and non-negotiables

Preserve a documented expressive architecture/applied-design branch, hierarchy through purposeful volumes or constructed graphic shape, and localized material/detail articulation connected to the whole. Brick color alone cannot establish identity. A plain rectangular building can still carry carefully organized projections/openings, just as a quiet cabinet can carry bowed sides and pointed hardware. Dense ornament, symmetry, curvature and historical paper wear are not requirements.

Use architecture/civic, furniture/lighting, patterned surfaces and associated editorial graphics as branch guidance, not four newly invented slugs. Gidding's museum-titled Art Deco designs are evidence of overlap within the applied-design context, not proof that Art Deco is a synonym. Wendingen's editorial system can frame unrelated reproduced subjects; attribution must remain separate.

### Sampling and stopping decision

The target and major contradictions are resolved through31 independently inspected works across institutional object collections, a letterform archive, a museum platform and attributed specialist reproductions. Architectural works include social housing, a commercial store, school, private villa, bridge and church interior; regional coverage exceeds Amsterdam. Applied works include five furniture designs, two clocks, ceramics, textile specimen/carpet and a complete lamp. Graphics include editorial issues/covers and Cohen examples. This is representative research, not an exhaustive movement inventory.

Later boundary checks sharpened rather than broadened mandatory traits: purely photographic advertising does not become School by artist name; cropped textile photos do not become complete specimens; altered architecture does not prove original colors. Stop collection after target plus these contradictions; further additions can enrich lesser-sampled ceramics, lighting and female applied designers without holding up this grounded entry. Historical UI behavior remains unknown regardless of corpus size.

### Interface interpretation

Proposed mode: contemporary revival. A fictional neighborhood walk planner can use three original conceptual facade drawings, a square editorial route sheet, a constructed display wordmark and clear practical stop metadata. Selecting/reordering stops should update a meaningful itinerary and abstract route diagram. Distances and places must be explicitly fictional unless verified real-route data is supplied; do not claim GPS navigation or transmit location. This is an architectural/editorial branch showcase, not a recreation of a historic publication or all media at once.

Use quiet readable controls outside the artwork, structural lines and consistent spacing to echo crafted integration. Contemporary colors, type sizes, radii and focus rules are proposed design values, not measured historical constants. Accessible legibility outranks imitation of a historical display letter at body-text size.

### Boundaries and anti-patterns

- Requiring all-red brick, all-rounded silhouettes, towers or densely ornate borders.
- Treating all Wendingen art, all work by Cohen/Gidding/Krop or every restored object as original-period School evidence.
- Copying source buildings' complete compositions, museum scans, sacred symbols or colonial portraits into original application assets.
- Calling the movement generic Art Deco, Art Nouveau or De Stijl; overlapping influences do not remove distinct context and expressive mass/material organization.
- Converting current decay into mandatory texture or inventing historical font, hex, timing and breakpoint measurements.

## Connections

- `art-nouveau`: organic ornament and crafted integration overlap; the School's expressive volume/material rhythm also supports angular and restrained variants.
- `art-deco`: shared period, applied-art geometry and decorative abstraction, explicitly acknowledged in Gidding objects. The School also has particular Dutch architectural/civic and editorial relationships.
- `de-stijl`: neighboring Dutch modernism; a rectilinear facade is insufficient to infer its flat-plane/orthogonal abstraction rules.
- `arts-and-crafts`: crafted material and integrated environments overlap; neither is a synonym or simple parent of the other.
- Wendingen is an associated publication; Dutch Expressionism is a broader relation, not a newly assigned alias.
