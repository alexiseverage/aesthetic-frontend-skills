### Visual evidence summary
- Modern board-game box art is not one look but a packaging regime: the cover must make a strong first impression on shelf/Kickstarter/store listings, communicate theme instantly, and imply the play experience. Source: Meeple Mountain; LaunchBoom.
- Stonemaier’s publisher-side guide shows how box covers sit within a larger system of landscape illustration, card illustration, component art, and rights/licensing; box art is both hero image and production constraint. Source: Stonemaier.
- LaunchBoom emphasizes worldbuilding, information clarity, and table-presence consistency, indicating that covers need to promise an internal visual system rather than stand alone as poster art. Source: LaunchBoom.
- BoardGameGeek’s current and historical cover ecosystem shows wide genre spread, but recurring commercial patterns: centered title, thematic tableau, polished genre signaling, and strong thumbnail readability. Source: BGG pages.

### Seven-dimension synthesis
- Palette
  - Canonical: genre-driven rather than universal; fantasy boxes lean saturated jewel tones, Euro/strategy boxes often use warm painterly or prestige-muted schemes, family games favor brighter approachable palettes.
  - Common: one dominant color story with high focal contrast for thumbnail recognition.
  - Variant: stark minimalist covers in boutique/art-game spaces.
- Type
  - Canonical: large title as brand object; genre-coded display face or custom logotype; subtitle/awards/supporting copy subordinate.
  - Common: embossed, outlined, metallic, or bannered title treatments for thematic reinforcement.
  - Variant: austere sans logotypes for modernist/abstract games.
- Texture
  - Canonical: polished illustration or composited paint/digital finish; print-ready clarity; enough detail to imply richness without dying at thumbnail size.
  - Common: atmospheric fog, parchment, brushed texture, cosmic glow, or tactile material simulation depending on genre.
  - Variant: flat graphic or collage-forward indie covers.
- Shape
  - Canonical: square box face organized around central tableau or emblem; title block top/center; icon clusters secondary.
  - Common: character groupings, horizon lines, map/emblem insets, frame devices.
  - Variant: abstract symbolic covers or borderless panoramic art.
- Motion
  - Canonical: implied narrative/action frozen at a legible moment; enough energy to provoke curiosity without obscuring title/readability.
  - Common: charged poses, exploration vectors, battle anticipation, environmental drama.
  - Variant: static contemplative tableau for pastoral or abstract strategy titles.
- Spatial
  - Canonical: foreground focal hook + background world context; the box must read at shelf distance and thumbnail size.
  - Common: poster-like depth with strong midground title integration.
  - Variant: extremely flat graphic compositions in family/light-party segments.
- Cultural markers
  - Canonical: theme telegraphy (fantasy realm, trains, civilizations, woodland coziness, sci-fi tech, historical map, etc.), publisher-quality sheen, and collector-box branding. Source: Meeple Mountain; LaunchBoom; Stonemaier.
  - Common: award laurels, player-count/age cues on edges, franchise or designer reputation cues.
  - Variant: retro-pastiche covers borrowing pulp, children’s-book, or art-poster vocabularies.

### Related-aesthetic boundaries
- Vs. `trading-card-game-design`: board-game box art is packaging-first and shelf-distance branding-first; TCG design is frame/UI-first around repeatable card objects.
- Vs. `fantasy-illustration`: many covers use fantasy illustration, but the board-game-box-art aesthetic is about packaging hierarchy and purchase conversion, not illustration style alone.
- Vs. `wpa-poster-style` or other poster aesthetics: board-game covers may borrow poster logic, but need product-brand legibility and thumbnail commerce performance.
- Vs. `corporate-memphis`: even family games rarely use pure product-UI flatness; they promise tactile world and table presence.

### Cultural-sensitivity / misuse cautions
- Avoid collapsing the category into only Euro-fan


## Direct media audit — 2026-11-12

This audit supersedes earlier generic dimension claims and limited source counts; earlier notes are retained as research history.
32 complete publisher-hosted covers/product renders were inspected individually: five Leder, nine Stonemaier, twelve Flatout and six Libellud. All 32 are distinct games; alternate editions, box spines and repeated series systems were not counted again. A 446 × 600 complete TEN original is small but legible. Cascadia’s filename says tease, but the inspected image is a complete square cover. A Gigamic access failure and obsolete Czech route were discovery attempts, not evidence. No originals were retained or reused.

This is a packaging meta-aesthetic, not one illustration style. Selection deliberately includes quiet white covers, abstract lettering, nature scenes, cartoon work, atmospheric fantasy, emblem covers and relief-like surfaces. Publisher concentration favors contemporary hobby publishing; it does not represent all countries, eras or mass-market packaging. Credited designers are not assumed to be illustrators. Stop: 32 complete works resolve the narrow old entry’s defining gaps; additional editions of these games would repeat rather than broaden evidence.

### Seven-dimension evidence and confidence

- Palette — high: BG01–32 vary from orange/teal and jewel colors to white/gray and black. Theme determines roles; a universal jewel palette is unsupported.
- Type — high: BG01–05, BG15–18, BG23–24, BG27–32 make title lettering a product identity. BG13 and BG09 leave extensive quiet ground. Exact fonts are unknown unless credits specify them; proposed interface stacks are substitutes.
- Texture — high: BG06–08, BG17–22, BG25–32 show watercolor, stipple, engraving, dry brush and relief illusions. Actual finish/foil cannot be established from a digital render.
- Shape — high: repeated square/tall box faces, framed nameplates, corner metadata and visible spines. BG23–24 establish abstract pattern/lettering alternatives to narrative tableaux.
- Spatial — high: BG01–32 organize one dominant promise, a title and secondary credits/icons. BG13 is sparse; BG19 is dense. No fixed density is canonical.
- Motion — unknown from still packaging. Implied narrative, racing diagonals and frozen poses are compositional cues, not measured animation or unboxing timing. A still interface is appropriate; future transitions are proposals.
- Cultural markers — moderate/high: visible author/illustrator credits, publisher marks, age/player/time metadata, shelf readability and theme-specific settings. Product claims and awards belong to real products; they must never be invented for a fictional showcase. Geography and cultural motifs require title-specific context.
