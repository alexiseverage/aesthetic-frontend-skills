# Mingei

Mingei is the twentieth-century folk-craft movement associated with Yanagi Sōetsu, Kawai Kanjirō and Hamada Shōji. Its usable visual framework connects beauty with use, material, process and repeated craft practice. It is a historically situated way of valuing and making objects, not a single uniform palette or a synonym for every handmade object. This profile distinguishes historical specimens collected as models, named movement practitioners, and explicitly documented international studio relationships.

**Evidence status:** thirty useful complete still works meet the ten-work floor and thirty-work working target. Maintainer self-review supports the local canonical entry; publication and the paired website release remain pending. Reference images are analytical links, not production assets. No animation, historical UI behavior, measured hex palette or recovered typeface is claimed.

## Analysis

### Claim-to-artifact map and contradictions

Beauty of use and material/process coherence is grounded in historical vessels 1–6, textiles 7–10, practical objects 16–20, and tables 27–28. Named makers extend those relationships through vessels 5–6 and 23–26, constructed lacquer 11–12, graphic craft activity 13–14, and applied screens/garments 15, 21–22 and 30. Movement context plus exact-work form/function supports this relationship; generic handmade appearance or artist membership alone is insufficient.

Pattern follows construction: stripes curve around the bowl in 2, slip occupies the serving field in 6, motifs cross garment panels in 7–10 and 29–30, and repeated modules align with screen folds in 21–22. This supports a useful layout translation with visible divisions and economical repetition. It does not require a universal asymmetry rule: deliberate repeated rows coexist with scattered shell and regional-map compositions.

Strong color, gloss and ornament occur in 3–6, 9, 11–12, 15, 20 and 23–30. They materially contradict beige-only, matte-only and plain-only definitions. Rich shell inlay and a patron-commissioned lamp in 11–12 complicate an inexpensive-only claim. The named graphic/studio branches cannot be described as universally anonymous mass household goods. Preserve the movement's ideals as historical aspirations, with those tensions visible rather than silently excluding inconvenient works.

Integrated lettering is a variant, supported by the exact character in 11, the source-identified syllables in 21, map marks in 15 and maker initials in 26. Seven blank shogi-piece silhouettes in 7 do not establish kanji typography. No historical font name is supplied by these images, and untranslated inscriptions should not become decorative UI gibberish.

### Regional identity and history

Historical Korean desks and paper furniture in 27–28 retain Joseon attribution. Their collecting context cannot retroactively make them Japanese products. The Shuri bingata garment in 29 retains Ryukyu Kingdom provenance; its wearer's rank and individual symbolic meanings remain unknown. Source text about Ainu craft does not authorize visual rules for uninspected motifs. Colonial collecting history and Yanagi's recorded opposition to oppression both belong in context; neither cancels the other.

Leach's 25–26 works have a documented British medieval-slipware revival context within an international Mingei-associated studio relationship. They are not Japanese geographic evidence. The pelican motif's source-supported symbolic meaning is specific, not a ready-made generic craft brand. Historical models from the sixteenth–nineteenth centuries predate Mingei's twentieth-century naming, and should not be backdated as movement-produced objects.

### Interface translation

A contemporary everyday-use composition tool can organize original useful objects and patterned cloth through construction-aware repetition. Concrete UI tokens, fonts, dimensions, breakpoints and interactions are proposals. Calm immediate feedback respects the still corpus without claiming an inherited historical motion system. Material cues should live in imagery and restrained seams; readable control surfaces and focus outlines remain clear.

One meaningful proposal is a meal-setting planner: choose a woven ground, place a bounded number of original bowls and cups, adjust their quantity, and see the setting and item count change together. This is a current fictional object set, not an authentic museum reconstruction, commercial craft listing or claim about Japanese dining customs. It should use original everyday form/process vocabulary without copying individual textile patterns, artist signatures or culturally specific motifs. Distinctness must be checked against Shaker's storage inventory and this batch's saved visual notebooks.

The target is met with useful ceramic, textile, graphic, metal, wood/lacquer, paper and regional variations. Remaining gaps are fine making methods, broader living-practitioner coverage and actual historical motion; none is silently filled by a generated showcase. Further research can enrich these branches without turning this bounded corpus into a universal definition of folk craft.

## Connections

- `arts-and-crafts`: shared beauty/use/material and reform-design concerns, with a documented international relationship. Mingei's twentieth-century Japanese institutions and source-traced regional models differ from mandatory British medieval/botanical book ornament.
- `shaker-design`: useful objects, material and repeat overlap. Mingei does not inherit Shaker religious communal storage logic, peg rails or compulsory restraint; decorated lacquer and saturated glaze belong.
- `wabi-sabi-slow-living`: humble material and some irregularity overlap. Impermanence, deliberate aging, tea lineage and contemplative incompletion are not mandatory Mingei conditions.
- `warm-minimalism`: some material colors overlap, but beige luxury editing and stealth wealth do not explain process, repetition or movement history.
- `scandinavian-modern`: functional craft and domestic scale overlap; different regional/institutional histories and varied motif/process relationships remain defining boundaries.
- Historical collected models, named movement practice, graphic/applied craft and the documented international studio relation are prose branches, not invented additional canonical slugs. Sashiko, bingata, kataezome, all Korean craft, all Ainu craft and all Leach works are not aliases for Mingei.
