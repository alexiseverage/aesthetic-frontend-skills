## Analysis

Chinoiserie is application-ready only when its signal is distributed across all seven dimensions rather than resting on palette alone. The load-bearing pattern is: lacquer/porcelain surface logic + asymmetrical ornamental framing + European exoticist connotation + elite decorative density. Frontend translation should begin with that pattern, then apply color, texture, shape, and motion as evidence of it.

Use as European chinoiserie or critical quotation, not as authentic Chinese design; avoid fake calligraphy, pan-Asian motif soup, and racialized exotic fantasy.

### Visual evidence summary
- The V&A collection page shows British 18th-century furniture, wallpapers, porcelain groups, and ornament prints translating Chinese, Japanese, and broader East Asian motifs into European decorative schemes rather than documenting direct East Asian originals. The object set includes lacquered/japanned furniture, blue-and-white ceramics, asymmetrical ornament, and printed pattern books by Pillement. (V&A)
- Britannica explicitly defines chinoiserie as fanciful European interpretation rather than accurate depiction, and ties it to Baroque/Rococo asymmetry, gilding, lacquering, blue-and-white ceramics, ornamental figures, and pagoda/garden forms. (Britannica)
- The Met’s exoticism essay places the style inside a larger history of European fantasies of the non-West, imperial trade, and decorative novelty. (Met, Exoticism)
- The Met’s 2025 "Monstrous Beauty" framing is critical for current use: chinoiserie also helped construct enduring racialized fantasies around Asian women and the "exotic East," so contemporary reuse cannot be treated as neutral ornament. (Met, Monstrous Beauty)

### Seven-dimension synthesis
Palette
- Canonical: lacquer black, deep red, porcelain white, cobalt blue, celadon/jade greens, and gilt accents, drawn from japanned furniture, export porcelain, and Rococo gilding. (V&A; Britannica)
- Common: coral, peach, pale green, and powdered pastels when the style merges with Rococo interiors or painted wallpaper. (Britannica; V&A)
- Variant: high-contrast noir-black interiors and 1920s/1930s revival palettes; use only if explicitly doing later revival rather than core 18th-century mode. (Britannica)

Type
- Historical chinoiserie is object- and surface-led more than typographic, so typography in a digital entry should be treated as translational synthesis rather than as a historical given. Based on ornament prints, lacquer panels, and export ceramics, the strongest direction is elegant serif display or restrained calligraphic accenting with high contrast and fine detail, never generic brush-script "Asian" pastiche. (V&A; Met, Monstrous Beauty)

Texture
- Canonical: lacquer/japanning gloss, porcelain sheen, gilded ornament, painted wallpaper, and carved/printed surface density. (V&A; Britannica)
- Common: silk and textile softness, screen-like panels, and reflective surfaces associated with elite interiors. (Britannica; Met, Exoticism)
- Avoid: faux-cracked parchment, pseudo-calligraphy, or texture libraries that imply "ancient East" generically; that reproduces exoticist flattening rather than historically specific European decorative borrowing. (Met, Monstrous Beauty)

Shape
- Canonical: asymmetrical cartouches, fret/lattice borders, pagoda silhouettes, scrolling branches, cranes, rocks, parasols, and porcelain vessel profiles. (Britannica; V&A)
- Common: curved mirrors, scalloped frames, pierced ornament, and nested room-within-room panels. (V&A)
- Variant: Regency bamboo, trellis, or pavilion-heavy revivals. (Britannica)

Motion
- Historical source material is static decorative art, but digital translation should feel slow, ceremonial, and drifting rather than kinetic or playful. Suggested motion language: reveal by curtain/panel, floating lantern-like transitions, delicate parallax on branch/trellis motifs. This is synthesis from the ornamental density and asymmetry of collection objects. (V&A; Britannica)

Spatial
- Canonical: layered surfaces, framed panels, inset scenes, and ornamented borders that turn walls, screens, cabinets, and beds into immersive decorative envelopes. (V&A; Britannica)
- Common: asymmetry inside otherwise aristocratic European room planning; the style often sits inside Baroque/Rococo spatial containers rather than replacing them wholesale. (Britannica)
- For web: think compartmentalized panels, border systems, and carefully staged focal objects rather than open modernist space.

Cultural markers
- Canonical signifiers: pagodas, pseudo-Chinese landscapes, export porcelain, lacquer, bamboo/trellis, birds, flowering branches, parasols, and imagined courtly figures. (Britannica; V&A)
- Critical marker for modern treatment: the style is inseparable from Europe

### Analysis and corrected boundary

The current audit establishes a varied object grammar rather than a mandatory dense red-and-gold skin. Narrow gilt edges, interrupted branches, dark lacquer fields, pale ceramic reserves and framed textile scenes recur. Painted frames often protect a conspicuously open center. The inspected revival fabrics also expose how European fantasy scenes and racialized caricatures persisted; decorative charm must not remove that context.

Chinese-made export objects, European imitations and later European revivals are not interchangeable categories. Identify the actual maker, place and market where known. A Chinese object collected in Europe does not thereby become European chinoiserie. The audit foregrounds European decorative quotations and explicitly excludes an ambiguous plain silver salver from qualifying identity evidence.

### Proposed frontend application

Use a framed folio, cabinet, drawer or inset-paper metaphor. Keep ordinary type and interaction labels separate from ornament. Suggested contemporary values, not historical measurements: warm paper #f2eadb, lacquer #3e282b, cobalt #2f5472, fine gilt #bca16c; 1–2px edge rules and spacious readable text reserves. Prefer static original botanical ornament and immediate selection/disclosure. Avoid pseudo-calligraphy, generic pan-Asian symbols, racialized figures and uncaptioned claims of authenticity.

### Limits and stop decision

The corpus is concentrated in British/French decorative collecting and institutional survival. It includes later textile revivals and varied media, not a statistically representative sample of all chinoiserie. Palette words and dimensions are qualitative visual observations; CSS values are proposed. No motion artifact was required for this intentionally still material tradition. Earlier generic profile percentages were not measured prevalence statistics; this dated audit supersedes that implication.

After the working target, additional textile, vessel/furniture and frame/publication variants reinforced the same distinctions. The defining material and spatial traits are sufficiently supported for a bounded contemporary translation; unobserved timing and underrepresented media remain explicitly unknown.
