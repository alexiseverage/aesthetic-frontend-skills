# Taishō Roman

## Scope and Source Context

A retrospective category for lyrical Japanese modern print and image culture centered on the Taishō period1912–1926, with late-Meiji roots and early-Shōwa continuities. Illustrated books, magazines, sheet music, postcards and decorative papers integrate expressive lettering, patterned textiles or flowers, slender figures and selective modern urban/Western references. It is not every object made in those years, every work by Yumeji, or a synonym for Japanese woodblock printing.

[MOMAT: Takehisa Yumeji—An Artist Who Defined His Era](https://www.momat.go.jp/en/exhibitions/570); [Nichibunken public image database announcement](https://www.nichibun.ac.jp/en/topics/db/2022/03/08/s001/); [Yayoi Museum: Takabatake Kashō](https://www.yayoi-yumeji-museum.jp/yayoi/exhibition/past_detail.html?id=2579); [Musashino: Kobayashi Kaichi exhibition](https://www.musashino.or.jp/museum/1002006/1002258/1002259/1002308/1002312.html); [Minatoya specialist gallery](https://yumeji-minatoya.co.jp/hpgen/HPB/entries/9.html).

MOMAT explicitly identifies Yumeji with Taisho Roman and places lyricism alongside Edo atmosphere, exoticism, urban sophistication and commercial goods. Its broad retrospective also includes political satire and earthquake documentation; those are not automatically this decorative branch. Yayoi describes Kashō's Nihonga and Western training, advertising work and magazine illustration; Nichibunken's public database spans1901–1941, substantially wider than this entry. Public images are those for which copyright has expired or permission was obtained; item-specific notices remain relevant. Musashino places Kaichi's Sakurai-ya stationery and postcards in later Taishō/early Shōwa publishing, discussing young women's postcard collecting, long figures, Western motifs and Art Deco/Futurist connections. These do not imply universal acceptance, prosperity or social freedom across the era.

Retain dates: T03 is a1911 edition with a1909 first edition, a precursor; T11/T12 are1931 and T15 is1929, later continuities. T02's early1910s dating straddles the era boundary. Most magazine references are1924–1926. Undated Kaichi objects retain the collection's broad period context without invented exact years.

## Analysis

### Boundary and Interpretation

The useful identity is the relationship between lyric figure/ornament, patterned plane and editorial frame. T06 is a decorative collar design without a portrait; T24 is a functional contents page; neither needs a melancholy woman at center. T16 combines Japanese and Western dress. T17/T19/T27/T29/T35 counter a kimono-only rule. T30 counters compulsory rich color. T03 is strongly botanical and graphic rather than photographic. Later works retain continuities without being backdated.

T04's1905 graveyard satire, T08/T09's c1923 landscapes and T13's1923 earthquake documentation are excluded because maker/period alone is insufficient. T10 is a published detail with inadequate complete-composition evidence. This is a classification choice for this entry, not a judgement on their importance. Current exhibitions use broad retrospective groupings; do not inherit every exhibited object into one style.

Art Nouveau shares organic contour and integrated lettering, but Taishō Roman requires its Japanese lyrical publishing context and specific pattern/image/title relationships. Art Deco overlaps Kaichi's angular theatrical branch without defining all Yumeji/Kashō work. Shin-hanga is a modern woodblock revival with a different production and subject framing; medium or period alone cannot collapse them. Generic Japonisme, contemporary anime and beige nostalgia are neighboring references, not aliases.

The Camellia Letter is a familiar newsletter form: original illustrated reader panel, distinct masthead and display headline, plain labeled name/email fields and a welcome-letter preview. ### Claim Status and Limits

Directly observed: all ledgered color masses, contours, layouts and print/painting surfaces at the stated resolutions. Source-supported: makers, titles, years/edition notes, collection attribution and broader publishing context. Inferred: common visual grammar and classification among neighbors. Proposed: all hexadecimal roles, local font choices, breakpoints, dimensions, field states and focus behavior. Unknown: exact historical inks/typefaces, production methods where not in metadata, intended symbolism for individual flowers/cards/churches, reception across all audiences, and animation timing.
