# Amsterdam School

Amsterdam School connects expressive architectural volumes, crafted applied objects and constructed graphic detail into a coherent whole. Its vocabulary includes sculptural masonry, shaped timber furniture, patterned surfaces, modeled metalwork and integrated lettering; neither red brick nor a particular curved silhouette is sufficient by itself.

## Scope

Use for architectural guides, civic cultural publishing, applied-art collections and editorial surfaces that can support a strong structural visual anchor. The Dutch movement is centered on approximately 1910–1930, with examples beyond Amsterdam and with social housing, commercial, domestic and religious commissions carrying different meanings.

Wendingen is an associated editorial strand, not a synonym or proof that every reproduced subject belongs to the movement. Furniture, lighting, patterned surfaces and graphic work extend the vocabulary beyond architecture. Artist membership alone does not classify every work; later Cohen advertisements and some Gidding designs overlap other modernist systems. A frontend is a contemporary interpretation, not an authenticated historical interface.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: Material relationships and selective accents carry identity: brown/red/yellow masonry against pale openings, dark or golden timber against upholstery, ink against paper. Violet, orange, turquoise and dark textile fields are established applied-art alternatives; a yellow/white church interior challenges mandatory terracotta. Choose a branch-specific palette. Restored paint and renewed upholstery do not automatically document original colors.

**Type**: Constructed display letters, altered stroke relationships, distinctive numerals and rules integrated with architectural or editorial structure. Thin and broad, angular and curved treatments coexist; supporting captions and prose can be conventional. No single font defines the movement. Expressive display type belongs at a readable scale, with plain support text.

**Texture**: Masonry courses, timber grain/slats, textile repeats, modeled relief and printed marks articulate chosen surfaces. Broad smooth planes can remain quiet. Apply detail locally rather than covering every component with rough brick or paper noise. Existing moss, stains and photographic compression are condition, not requirements.

**Shape**: Expressive volumes and joins: bowed sides, faceted bays, stepped towers, shaped openings, unusual negative spaces and repeated constructed motifs. Symmetry and asymmetry, restrained rectangles and organic contours are all valid. Choose a coherent geometry system; an arch, tower or rounded corner is optional.

**Motion**: Sources are static architecture, objects and graphics. Historical UI timing and easing are unknown. Intentional stillness and immediate control updates are suitable proposed behavior; any optional transition must remain nonessential and honor reduced motion. Avoid autonomous rotating ornaments or simulated masonry movement.

**Spatial**: A dominant volume or image connects to repeated secondary structures and quieter zones. Square editorial framing is useful in the Wendingen branch, while architecture and furniture use many proportions. Integrated design means related hierarchy and detail, not decoration everywhere or universally dense composition.

**Cultural markers**: Specific Dutch expressive architecture/applied-design context, crafted total environments, municipal housing and information, architectural lettering and associated periodical design. Workers' housing, private villas, stores and churches have different social purposes. Jewish/socialist designers, religious symbols and maritime colonial imagery need their own attribution and context.

## Non-Negotiables

**Non-negotiables**:

- A grounded architectural, applied-art or associated graphic branch.
- Purposeful expressive mass, negative space or constructed graphic shape that survives a palette change.
- Material/detail rhythm and hierarchy connected to the larger composition.

Terracotta, curvature, symmetry, allover ornament and visible aging are common or variant signals, not universal rules.

## Connotation

**Mode:** contemporary revival.

Suggest crafted integration, expressive structure and attention to everyday environments. Civic housing can evoke collective care; a private villa or costly lamp has a different register. Avoid turning “workers' palaces” into a generic luxury slogan or treating every decorative source as socially equivalent.

## Related / Subsets

- `art-nouveau`: organic ornament and integrated craft overlap; Amsterdam School also supports angular, structural and restrained variants.
- `art-deco`: shared period and decorative geometry, including explicit overlap in Gidding applied objects. The Dutch architectural/civic and editorial context remains specific.
- `de-stijl`: neighboring Dutch modernism; rectilinear shapes alone do not imply its orthogonal color-plane system.
- `arts-and-crafts`: material craft and integrated environments overlap, without a synonym or simple parent relationship.
- Architectural/civic, furniture/lighting, patterned-surface and Wendingen-associated editorial strands are guidance branches, not additional canonical slugs. Dutch Expressionism is a broader relation, not an alias.

## Frontend / UI Guidance

Use a strong original structural illustration or shaped panel as the anchor, with repeated rules or stepped divisions connecting title, practical metadata and controls. A square route sheet beside a conceptual facade can suit an architecture guide; a textile specimen needs different imagery and pattern density. Functional labels remain plain and readable.

Keep state meaningful: selecting or arranging stops should update an itinerary and diagram, rather than only recoloring cards. Clearly identify fictional route data and local demonstration state. Provide visible selection, keyboard focus, adequate touch targets and text alternatives for diagrams. Preserve a coherent geometry rhythm as the layout stacks on narrow screens.

## CSS Translation

- Proposed architecture/editorial roles: paper `#F0E6CF`, ink `#252A28`, masonry `#9B4937`, warm accent `#D4A64B`, pale line `#C2B79F`. These are contemporary application choices, not sampled pigments or a universal palette.
- Use original SVG volumes, small brick-course or slat marks and authored constructed lettering. Keep strong pattern inside art or selected dividers, away from body text and controls.
- Use thin structural rules with limited heavier emphasis; combine rectangular controls with selectively stepped or bowed artwork. Do not assign a different decorative shape to every control.
- Prefer a deliberate panel grid with substantial padding and little shadow. Collapse to one column while preserving artwork proportions and the hierarchy of heading, diagram and itinerary.
- Make native controls immediate with explicit focus and selection indicators. Disable optional nonessential motion under `prefers-reduced-motion`; stillness is valid by default.

## Typography / Fonts

Use a small original constructed wordmark or display alphabet for the main anchor. Its angular/curved stroke relationships should belong to the chosen branch rather than imitate a particular artist's signature. Pair with a system sans for practical text or a readable serif such as Georgia for editorial support; these are proposed contemporary choices. No historical typeface identity is established by those fallbacks. Avoid elaborate display treatment on inputs, long prose or tiny map labels.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

Attribute historical buildings, objects, makers and sources. Museum scans, historical compositions, seals and carved figures need separate rights handling; original assets must have their own provenance. Distinguish original materials from restoration, replacement and present weathering. Do not silently reuse sacred motifs, colonial explorers or ethnic caricatures as general decoration. Preserve specific social and religious context without claiming a uniform political intent for every commission.

## Anti-Patterns

- A brick-colored template with arches but no structural hierarchy or material articulation.
- Mandatory all-curved silhouettes, towers, dark rooms or dense ornament.
- Treating all Wendingen images or every work by an associated artist as Amsterdam School.
- Calling the movement generic Art Deco, Art Nouveau or De Stijl.
- Copying historical layouts, carvings or source photographs into supposedly original showcase art.
- Presenting restored colors as universal historical measurements, or inventing exact fonts, motion and responsive rules from still images.
