# Cyanotype

Cyanotype is a photographic process and the visual language of its blue image-bearing surface: pale contact impressions or continuous photographic tones within iron-salt-coated paper. Developed by John Herschel in 1842, it connects botanical albums, technical reproduction and living experimental photographic practice. A blue botanical illustration can evoke it, but color and plant motifs alone do not reproduce its relationship between light, contact, image and paper.

## Scope

Use for photographic portfolios, specimen albums, field-note collections, print studios, cultural archives and carefully paced editorial experiences. Choose a documented branch: botanical contact printing, photographs made from negatives, experimental layered photograms, or technical/documentary reproduction. These share a process rather than one compulsory subject or layout.

The digital translation should show an image formed on or within a light-sensitive sheet. A pale fern silhouette is one strong branch; portraits, buildings, anatomical images, maps, landscapes and mixed-media works are also documented. Do not present an original digital illustration as a historical print or a scientifically verified specimen record.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: Prussian-blue-like darks, cyan-blue midtones and pale unexposed forms or highlights; white or cream paper may remain outside the coated field. The inspected prints range from muted blue-gray to saturated blue, with both luminous and very dark compositions. Mixed media may add other colors, and toning can extend the process beyond the untoned blue branch. Screen hex values are proposed application values, not chemical specifications or measurements from museum scans.

**Type**: Many images have little or no lettering. Botanical albums often carry small handwritten names beneath a specimen; technical reproductions can include fine labels, grids, diagrams and annotations. Let the image lead. Use a readable serif for album prose, quiet sans or monospace for modern utility, and restrained italic or handwriting-like captions when appropriate. No universal cyanotype typeface exists.

**Texture**: Image and paper are inseparable: granular midtones, coating variation, pale contact traces, softened photographic detail and sometimes visible brush boundaries. Some works have crisp rectangular image edges and clean sheets; others expose ragged coating or deckled paper. Brown aging, scratches, tape and heavy stains are not compulsory process characteristics. Keep reading text clear of dense texture.

**Shape**: A rectangular sheet or coated image field contains organic contact silhouettes, photographic scenes, or fine reproduced lines. Contact can preserve extremely thin stems, serrated leaves, roots and translucent overlaps; prints from negatives retain continuous volume. Distinguish the image boundary, the coating boundary and the paper boundary rather than collapsing them into one decorative frame.

**Motion**: The collected artifacts are still prints; no historical timing or easing is established. Intentional stillness is appropriate. Contemporary album selection, image replacement or an explicitly labeled process illustration can respond immediately to user action. Do not imply that a screen slider accurately simulates exposure chemistry, and avoid a permanent blue shimmer or floating-leaf loop.

**Spatial**: A single specimen with ample ground and a subordinate caption is common in botanical plates, including asymmetrical diagonals and unusually fine negative spaces. Other branches use photographic cropping, densely layered impressions, full-sheet blue, or technical montage. Preserve the chosen branch's image-to-sheet relationship. Repeated dashboard cards are not required merely because the source is a collection.

**Cultural markers**: Herschel's photographic process, Anna Atkins's botanical publishing, contact impressions, blue image-bearing paper, handwritten specimen names, photographic negatives, and technical reproduction. Contemporary artists extend the medium through portraiture, embodiment, landscape and collage. A botanical branch should acknowledge Atkins without suggesting that she invented the process or that all cyanotypes are Victorian nature studies.

## Non-Negotiables

**Non-negotiables**: a convincing image-on-coated-sheet relationship; contact-derived pale forms or coherent photographic/technical tonal reproduction; material evidence appropriate to the selected branch; image and paper hierarchy that survives without a decorative plant icon. For the untoned blue branch, keep a coherent blue tonal family rather than arbitrary duotone coloring.

## Connotation

**Mode:** contemporary revival, with explicit historical attribution when quoting an album tradition.

The medium can read as attentive, experimental, intimate, documentary or scientific. It is an active photographic practice as well as a historical process. A sun-print album can be quiet without becoming cottage fantasy, melancholy without implying spiritual evidence, and precise without claiming the measurements of an engineering plan.

## Related / Subsets

- `risograph`: both reveal production and paper; Risograph uses stencil-duplicated spot inks, overprint and registration, while Cyanotype records light through contact or a negative.
- `autumn-field-guide-naturalism`: shares observation and labels; Cyanotype is defined by photographic printing rather than seasonal field-guide imagery, warm autumn pigments or identification-task structure.
- `cottagecore`: botanical subjects overlap, but domestic pastoral romance is optional. A tonal architectural cyanotype need not be pastoral at all.
- `spirit-photography-seance`: may share photographic ghosting and historical print context; cyanotype process and multiple impressions do not imply séances, supernatural claims or theatrical evidence.
- Botanical albums, negative-based photographs, experimental photograms and documentary/technical reproduction are useful branches, not separate canonical slugs here.

## Frontend / UI Guidance

Build an album spread, photographer's print index, specimen folio or process notebook. Give the primary sheet enough room to reveal fine edges and tonal detail. Place description, credit, title and controls on quieter paper areas. Browsing should change the actual selected image and its caption, with a stable reading order and meaningful accessible labels.

Avoid pretending that generated plants are authenticated botanical specimens. If a process demonstration is included, label it as an interpretation and provide a still endpoint. Text remains real HTML, even when the main image contains expressive photographic lettering.

## CSS Translation

- Proposed blue-branch roles: paper `#F3F1E7`, ink `#173F65`, exposed blue `#174C7D`, pale trace `#DBE7E4`, muted ink `#456079`. These are contemporary UI tokens, not sampled museum colors.
- Use an original raster print image for organic contact detail and coating texture. Restrained SVG can supply a keyline or margin notation; avoid repeating one leaf icon as the entire visual system.
- Keep paper surfaces almost flat, with square corners and at most a faint binding or sheet shadow. Ornamental rounded cards and heavy floating glows break the physical premise.
- Distinguish a blue coated rectangle within the paper from the outer paper margin. A rough boundary is a variant, not a mandate for every component.
- Use a broad image column and a smaller annotation column, collapsing into image, caption, notes and controls on narrow screens. Preserve natural image proportions and avoid clipping specimen tips.
- Keep text on calm paper; do not put small blue-gray labels into a mottled dark print. Use a visible border and text for selection, underlined links, strong focus outlines and adequate control targets.
- Prefer immediate still state changes. Reduced motion should preserve all content and selection functionality.

## Typography / Fonts

Use ordinary readable book typography for the interface: a restrained serif with italic captions and a clean utility face. Handwritten naming in source albums supports a treatment, not an identifiable universal font. Avoid decorative calligraphy throughout, excessively tracked tiny capitals, or simulated exposure effects on body text. A digital album's larger type scale is an accessibility adaptation, not a claim about historical page measurements.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

Credit the process and individual makers. Atkins's scientific access and publishing occurred within a specific nineteenth-century social and economic setting; avoid an isolated-genius story that removes collaborators or the conditions that funded her work. Respect museum image rights and living artists' work. Create original application imagery or use explicitly licensed assets rather than redistributing research reproductions. No chemical safety or exposure instructions are implied by this visual specification.

## Anti-Patterns

- A generic white fern icon on blue with no photographic or paper relationship.
- Treating every cyanotype as a botanical photogram; negatives, figurative work and technical reproduction are documented.
- Mandatory yellowed parchment, burn marks, stains, taped corners or grunge.
- Riso halftone dots and misregistered fluorescent inks substituted for light-formed detail.
- Calling every blue technical interface a cyanotype or implying a digital treatment is a real physical print.
- Fake Latin species names, invented archive accession numbers or inaccurate identification claims.
- Unsolicited exposure animation, permanent shimmer, or tiny handwritten utility labels.
