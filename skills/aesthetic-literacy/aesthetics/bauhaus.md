# Bauhaus

## Scope

Bauhaus is interwar German workshop modernism translated into geometric reduction, industrial honesty, functional hierarchy, and teaching-through-form. Use it for rational product surfaces, educational modernism, modular devices, fabrication/lab contexts, and interfaces that should expose construction rather than decorate it. It is not generic startup minimalism or any composition that merely uses primary colors.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: Primary red, yellow, and blue with black, white, gray, and restrained industrial neutrals. Color should act as structural signal or pedagogical demonstration, not mood wash.

**Type**: Geometric sans-serif, modular lettering, lower-case experimentation, simple captions, and hierarchy treated as functional composition. Type can become form, but should remain clear.

**Texture**: Glass, tubular steel, painted plaster, plywood, smooth paper, workshop textiles, and other materials shown with industrial honesty. Avoid faux luxury finish.

**Shape**: Circles, squares, triangles, rectilinear frames, visible modules, axes, and components derived from workshop and machine logic. Diagonals may create instructional tension, but decoration should be minimal.

**Motion**: Purposeful and functional: reveal construction, indicate assembly, or move through a clear learning hierarchy. Avoid decorative bounce or ornamental flourish.

**Spatial**: Asymmetric grid, generous whitespace, modular relationships, diagonal tension where useful, and object-like components with clear roles. Balance abstraction with production discipline.

**Cultural markers**: Kandinsky color-form theory, Bayer typography, Breuer furniture language, workshop pedagogy, modular teaching diagrams, and industrial material legibility.

## Non-Negotiables

**Non-negotiables**: Geometric primary forms, function-led hierarchy, material honesty, and modular composition are required. Without those, the entry collapses into vague geometric minimalism.

## Connotation

Bauhaus reads as rational, optimistic, educational, disciplined, and modern. Historically it belongs to interwar German modernism, workshop pedagogy, mass-production debates, and migration under political pressure; its clarity should feel principled rather than corporate-sanitized.

## Related / Subsets

Related: `de-stijl` is stricter about orthogonal abstraction and pure planar color; `ulm-school` extends Bauhaus rationalism into postwar systems design; `constructivism` shares avant-garde geometry but is more agitational and political. Subsets may emphasize workshop pedagogy, modular robotics/fabrication, or graphic primary-form posters.

## Frontend / UI Guidance

Build layouts from visible modules: cards, grids, labels, and controls should reveal their geometric roles. Use primary accents sparingly for state and hierarchy. Keep navigation, onboarding, and data displays instructional, direct, and materially legible.

## CSS Translation

Use CSS grid, strong alignment, primary-color blocks, black/white framing, flat color planes, thin or bold rule lines, and simple transform-based assembly motions. Favor matte color and hard edges; avoid skeuomorphic shadows, glass blur, decorative gradients, and luxury texture.

## Typography / Fonts

Use geometric sans families or broadly available geometric/humanist sans fallbacks. Keep weights intentional, spacing clean, and labels functional. Do not use ornate serif, script, blackletter, or nostalgic display as the system voice.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

Avoid presenting Bauhaus as ahistorical “clean design.” It is tied to specific educational, industrial, political, and migration histories. Do not erase workshop craft or the pressure that forced its closure and dispersal.

## Anti-Patterns

Do not make every surface a primary-color toy, confuse Bauhaus with generic Memphis geometry, hide all construction behind Apple-like seamlessness, or add decorative motion unrelated to function.
