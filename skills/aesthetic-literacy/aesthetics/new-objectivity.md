# New Objectivity / Neue Sachlichkeit

Weimar-era unsentimental realism: sober observation, sharp social detail, restrained typography, clinical light, and class-conscious critique that rejects expressionist mysticism and decorative escape.

## Scope

Use this aesthetic only where its full visual system supports the product, editorial, cultural, or institutional context. Keep the translation anchored in the source profile instead of extracting isolated decorative motifs.

Origin context: New Objectivity emerged in Germany after World War I as artists rejected Expressionism for sober, often satirical realism attentive to war trauma, social inequality, bureaucracy, and urban modernity.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: muted urban neutrals, tobacco brown, newsprint gray, black ink, sickly green, brick red, cream paper, and low-saturation flesh tones; contrast is hard but not glamorous.

**Type**: functional serif or grotesk captions, newspaper columns, case-file labels, sober exhibition text, typewriter or bureaucratic forms where appropriate; avoid heroic propaganda lettering.

**Texture**: newsprint, dry brush, photographic grain, worn plaster, ink wash, ledger paper, municipal forms, and matte surfaces with documentary roughness.

**Shape**: hard-edged figures, cropped frames, rectangles, case panels, sharp profiles, street windows, office forms, and observational compositions with little ornamental curvature.

**Motion**: minimal and reportorial: cut, reveal, scroll, cross-reference, or case-file expansion; motion should feel like investigation, not spectacle.

**Spatial**: documentary grids, dossiers, street-level observation, tight columns, evidence panels, and layouts that create social comparison rather than escapist depth.

**Cultural markers**: Weimar Republic, Otto Dix, George Grosz, Christian Schad, social realism, war trauma, urban class critique, journalism, cabaret-era unease, and anti-romantic objectivity.

## Connotation

**Mode:** contemporary revival.

- **Origin context**: New Objectivity emerged in Germany after World War I as artists rejected Expressionism for sober, often satirical realism attentive to war trauma, social inequality, bureaucracy, and urban modernity.
- **Contemporary reading**: Today it reads as investigative, severe, socially literate, and historically uneasy; it should not be reduced to “cold vintage Germany.”
- **Audience resonance**: Art-history audiences recognize Weimar painting and print culture; broader audiences register documentary severity, case-file structure, and social critique.
- **Fatigue / misuse risk**: Misuse risk is ideological flattening: making it look authoritarian, fascist, or merely noir while erasing Weimar trauma and class critique.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

- **Context:** New Objectivity is historically sensitive because it comes from post-WWI Germany, urban poverty, disability, veterans, sexuality, class conflict, and the fragile Weimar Republic before Nazi repression.
- **Use with care:** Use with care when referencing war injury, poverty, gender, sex work, or political extremism; do not aestheticize suffering.
- **Safer translation:** Translate documentary structure, sober palette, and critical observation into respectful information design.
- **Avoid:** Avoid fascist/authoritarian visual cues, caricatured German severity, trauma-as-texture, or flattening social critique into noir mood.
