# Edo Yōkai Woodblock

## Scope

A bounded supernatural picture tradition within Edo woodblock publishing, rather than an autonomous movement or a synonym for all ukiyo-e. Printed monster books, theatrical apparitions and supernatural narrative sheets combine emphatic contours, differentiated printed color or monochrome marks, purposeful image/text panels, and surprising relationships between ordinary and extraordinary bodies or objects. The combination supports a separately useful application entry; a monster subject alone does not.

The [Japan Foundation's Yōkai Parade](https://www.jpf.go.jp/e/project/culture/exhibit/traveling/yokai_parade.html) and [Edo-Tokyo Museum's exhibition](https://www.edo-tokyo-museum.or.jp/en/s-exhibition/daiyokai/) establish a broader history across media. This entry deliberately narrows that history to printed Edo examples. [Ōta Museum distinguishes Yoshitoshi's 1865 series from his 1889–1892 work](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/specters-by-tsukioka-yoshitoshi/); later Meiji pictures, painted monster scrolls, masks and contemporary manga need their own classification.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: Cream paper, strong dark contour and locally differentiated fields. Indigo/black night grounds with pale apparitions are common, but orange bursts, yellow interiors, green floors, gray open fields and monochrome books are supported variants. Controlled gradation can sit within otherwise flat print fields. Exact pigments and calibrated colors are not established by ordinary scans; oxidized white in particular works must not be canonized as intentional mottling.

**Type**: Carved Japanese inscriptions differentiate title, story, name, signature and publisher/censor information. Lobed labels, vertical rectangular strips, inset text blocks and separate top registers coexist. Some writing is integrated into narrative action. Translate hierarchy through actual readable content; use verified Japanese only when needed, never invented characters or copied prayers.

**Texture**: Strong outline plus selective fine hair, textile, wave, grass and hatching marks; broad fields prevent every area becoming equally noisy. Monochrome illustrated books use fine strokes and blank paper as well as dense drawing. Printed irregularity can be subtle. Stains, torn paper, discoloration and coarse artificial grunge are not required.

**Shape**: Expressive human/animal/object silhouettes; large heads, extended or transformed bodies, curling pale forms and abrupt shifts of scale. Small humans may confront immense apparitions, but near-human-scale spirits and tiny supernatural animals also occur. Patterned clothes, diagonal beams and curved boundaries frame the action. Do not make one eye, a lantern or a giant skeleton compulsory.

**Motion**: These are still printed works. Flight, smoke, transformation and storytelling do not supply measured animation timing. Intentional stillness and immediate user-driven changes suit the print quotation. No mandatory drifting ghost, flashing apparition, animated eye or scroll-linked reveal; retain all content and controls under reduced motion.

**Spatial**: Tall single sheets, horizontal multi-sheet scenes and book spreads all occur. Dense pattern contrasts with broad dark or pale fields; diagonal architecture and strongly unequal figures establish hierarchy. Title/narrative cartouches may frame or overlap a picture. Separate quiet functional zones from expressive artwork in software, and preserve whole-image framing on mobile rather than hiding essential action in a crop.

**Cultural markers**: Edo publishing, named artists/publishers, kabuki and literary narratives, regional stories and Japanese/Chinese narrative exchanges. Yōkai is a broad category; yūrei and named human ghosts have specific histories. Some scenes depict theatrical tricks rather than literal spirits. Religious inscriptions and objects, violent revenge and childbirth/death narratives require context, not generic spooky decoration.

## Non-Negotiables

**Non-negotiables**:

- Supernatural narrative remains tied to a specific Edo printed-picture context, or an explicitly new quotation of it.
- Controlled contour and fine local marks organize flat fields and patterned detail.
- Figure/object relationships and image/text hierarchy create a purposeful composition beyond a themed background.
- Actual textual content stays readable and meaningful; no arbitrary Japanese glyphs, seals or sacred emblems.

Blue, darkness, monstrous anatomy, decorative cartouches and distress are each insufficient on their own and none is universally mandatory.

## Connotation

**Mode:** contemporary quotation.

Can convey strange encounters, theatrical surprise, witty transformation and printmaking craft. Suitable for cultural exhibitions, illustrated publishing, event reservations and curated collections. A small booking form can inherit the picture/label hierarchy while retaining ordinary controls. Use restraint in dense productivity software; aggressive monstrous imagery can undermine reassurance or obscure errors.

## Frontend / UI Guidance

Use a strongly framed illustration beside a quiet reservation form, event program, product information or manual plate viewer. Let short labels and a distinct title panel set hierarchy. Keep functional copy in normal reading direction; historical vertical Japanese columns do not justify rotating English labels. Associate a large image with a real content unit rather than treating it as wallpaper.

Forms need explicit labels, native radio/quantity controls, clear constraints, inline errors, an error focus target and an announced confirmation. A booking demonstration should disclose its local status and avoid implying an actual paid reservation. Let changing selections update a meaningful summary. On narrow screens stack complete artwork, description and controls; keep labels wrapping and at least44px targets. These are proposed modern patterns, not recovered historical behaviors.

## CSS Translation

Proposed contemporary tokens, not sampled pigments: paper `#f3e7ce`, surface `#fff7e5`, ink `#182d35`, muted `#4c5b59`, vermilion `#963d2e`, indigo `#193f53`, rule `#7e806d`, ocher `#d3ab64`. Use quiet solid surfaces behind inputs and text; verify actual state pairs. Square sheet edges, thin dark rules, limited double borders and one colored title block can translate print organization without fake official stamps.

A responsive grid can give the illustration roughly one third to one half of the width while keeping form controls comfortable. Use `object-fit:contain` and intrinsic aspect ratio. Pattern belongs mainly within original artwork, not across validation messages. Disable decorative transitions, including inherited ones, when choosing stillness; no script-generated noise or random SSR details.

## Typography / Fonts

A readable contemporary serif for the event title and system sans for controls are explicit substitutes, not historical type identifications. Keep prose around16px with1.5 line height and short metadata slightly smaller. Real Japanese content requires correct wording, language tagging and an appropriate font with known coverage. No brush-font Latin body text, pseudo-calligraphy, arbitrary vertical kana or museum signature turned into branding.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

Distinguish imagined new spirits from named folkloric or religious figures. Do not turn Oiwa's suffering, funerary tablets, Buddhist prayer text or a manji into a playful logo or status badge. Human ghosts are not interchangeable object mascots. The [Mia Oiwa record](https://collections.artsmia.org/art/65717/the-ghost-of-oiwa-katsushika-hokusai) supplies a particular theatrical history; it does not authorize a single universal origin claim for all tales.

Attribute actual artists, publishers, titles and dates with the institution's qualifications. Later impressions and alternate scans are not new designs. Linked reference analysis and publication rights are different questions; original generated application art has separate provenance and is not historical evidence or a recovered Edo print.

## Anti-Patterns

- Generic anime monster art, a blue gradient or a Japanese word without printed composition.
- Treating all ukiyo-e, yōkai, yūrei, kabuki ghosts and Meiji prints as the same category.
- Requiring night blue, giant scale, one-eyed lanterns, torn paper or coarse grain despite observed alternatives.
- Copying religious inscriptions, seals or a named victim's face as decorative branding.
- Tiny rotated labels, auto-playing carousels, flashing ghost effects or textured error fields.
- Counting triptych parts, book views, later impressions and mirror scans as independent works.
