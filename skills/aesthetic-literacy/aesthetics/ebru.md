# Ebru

Ebru is a living Turkish marbling tradition in which pigments are arranged on a prepared water surface and transferred to a sheet. Rounded floating color cells, fine separated bands, drawn-out floral forms and deliberately combed repeats can all belong to its language. The finished surface records a balance of material behavior and practiced control. It is a named craft and transmission tradition, not a synonym for every colorful liquid image.

## Scope

Use for paper studios, craft exhibitions, book arts, pattern collections and thoughtfully framed digital studies of marbling. Choose a branch rather than combining every technique: Battal's scattered cellular fields, Gelgit's directional back-and-forth pulls, Taraklı's combed rhythm, Şal's flowing bands, Hatip motifs, or floral compositions. Named variants are not interchangeable recipes, and a digital interpretation is not a substitute for instruction by a practitioner.

Ebru's historical book and calligraphic uses coexist with independent contemporary artworks. Some compositions fill the sheet; others form borders, repeated small motifs or a single flower on a quiet ground. Japanese Suminagashi, other regional marbling traditions and modern acrylic fluid painting are related comparisons, not alternate names for this particular tradition. Do not assign all marbled paper to Turkey or compress complex histories into one invention story.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: Pigment colors remain locally distinct: blue, green, red, ochre, cream and dark contours recur in the corpus, but neither rainbow nor blue-only color is mandatory. Muted historical reproductions, red-dominant Battal, pale floral grounds and monochrome reproductions complicate a single palette. Use separated pigment bands and clear tonal layers rather than soft multicolor glow. Exact screen colors are application choices, not universal historical standards.

**Type**: Many sheets contain no lettering beyond a small signature. In book and calligraphic work, meaningful writing can occupy a quieter center, sit within marbled letter forms, or be framed by patterned borders. Calligraphy has linguistic and sometimes sacred content; it is not a decorative font category to imitate indiscriminately. Use readable contemporary utility typography for controls and give craft names, diacritics and maker credits proper treatment.

**Texture**: Floating pigment islands, tiny pale-rimmed drops, hairline channels, striation and occasional sandy/crackled pigment structure. The transferred surface is flat, with local density changes and physical paper presence. Brushed paint strokes, oil-paint impasto, holographic gloss, stains and generic digital grain do not automatically express Ebru. Later illustration can coexist with a marbled ground without proving that every mark was formed on water.

**Shape**: Nested rounded cells pressed against one another; pinched hearts or rosettes; thin extended pigment threads; tapering stems and leaves; repeated scalloped comb bands; irregular S-shaped sweeps. The chosen technique organizes these forms. Battal should not become a uniform bubble grid, and Taraklı should retain a tool-like repeat rather than dissolve into arbitrary waves.

**Motion**: Making is an active material process; finished sheets are still records. The still corpus establishes no exact gesture speed, easing or drying behavior. A contemporary workbench may show user-triggered drops, pulls or a transfer, clearly labeled as a digital study. Keep a stable printable endpoint, avoid compulsory ambient movement, and offer equivalent buttons for gesture-only actions. Reduced motion must preserve the pattern and all actions.

**Spatial**: All-over fields can preserve several scales of cells or fine repeated bands. Floral and Hatip branches introduce a focal shape or regular ornamental arrangement; mounted compositions can alternate dense borders with calm centers. Keep functional copy outside visually dense marbling. Preserve the difference between a working tray, a transferred sheet and an ornamental frame rather than placing every component inside the same busy texture.

**Cultural markers**: Turkish craft terminology, paper and book arts, master–apprentice transmission, named makers and lineages, the marbling tray, and controlled pigment manipulation. UNESCO inscribed Ebru in 2014. The tradition has meaningful cultural and sometimes spiritual contexts, but every secular pattern need not carry religious text, mosque imagery or a generalized “Eastern” atmosphere.

## Non-Negotiables

**Non-negotiables**: separated floating-pigment relationships rather than blended color alone; recognizable organization from a selected marbling branch; a surface/transfer or paper-art premise; attribution to a living named tradition instead of anonymous exotic ornament. Preserve fine boundaries and differences in scale.

## Connotation

**Mode:** contemporary craft interpretation, or authentic presentation when displaying properly credited practitioner work.

Ebru can feel attentive, vivid, disciplined, contemplative or experimental. Chance does not eliminate skill, and visual complexity is not a reason to call it chaotic. A respectful interface can invite exploration while making clear that its digital patterns do not establish mastery of the physical craft.

## Related / Subsets

- `cyanotype`: both connect a physical process to a sheet; Cyanotype records light through contact or a negative, while Ebru transfers arranged floating pigments.
- `risograph`: shared paper/material awareness, but stencil inks, overprint and registration are different production signals.
- `organic-digital`: shares fluid-looking forms; its soft-tech blobs and blended gradients lack Ebru's craft context, fine pigment separation and named pattern organization.
- `psychedelic`: can share saturated curves and optical complexity; Ebru does not require concert-poster distortion, countercultural symbolism or unreadable type.
- `art-nouveau`: floral curves can overlap; a marbled flower is formed within a pigment system rather than defined by architectural whiplash ornament or lithographic drawing.
- Battal, Gelgit, Taraklı, Şal, Hatip, floral, sandy and other named techniques are documented internal branches, not separate canonical slugs here. This entry is not an exhaustive teaching taxonomy.

## Frontend / UI Guidance

Build a print-studio workbench, pattern viewer, book-art exhibition or framed paper collection. Establish the selected branch in the main artwork before applying it to supporting elements. Controls can select a technique, compare a field, add an interpreted pigment mark, undo a step or keep a transferred sheet. Labels should explain what changes without claiming chemical accuracy.

Make gestures optional: include native buttons and a clear keyboard route. Keep small labels on stable plain areas, show selected controls with text and borders as well as color, and preserve a meaningful still state. A modern utility face is appropriate when the pattern carries the identity; decorative pseudo-calligraphy is not required.

## CSS Translation

- Proposed studio roles: warm paper `#F0E5D2`, dark ink `#302D29`, vermilion `#AB3F2F`, pigment blue `#245F75`, ochre `#D6A34B`, cream `#F5E8BD`. These are contemporary proposals, not sampled pigment recipes.
- Render original separated pigment fields with carefully authored SVG, canvas, a shader or original raster artwork. Keep hairline bands, nested cell structure and the selected technique legible; do not substitute a blurred mesh gradient.
- Reserve dense pattern for a tray, print or border. Let adjacent controls and reading surfaces remain calm.
- Use square paper edges and modest tool-like control geometry. Any tray or binding shadow should clarify the artifact, not imply floating glass panels.
- Keep body text at readable scale and provide strong focus outlines. Small decorative cell details may simplify on mobile; control labels and the full composition should survive.
- Implement explicit still endpoints and immediate reduced-motion state changes. A digital transfer effect is proposed interaction, not measured historical behavior.

## Typography / Fonts

Use a restrained readable serif for an exhibition or clear sans for a working studio. Contrast scale, weight and spacing rather than forcing ornament into every letter. Preserve Turkish characters in names and techniques. Historical calligraphic panels are specific artworks with specific content, not licenses to paste arbitrary Arabic script into buttons, logos or loading indicators.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

Credit practitioners and distinguish a documented work from a pattern study. Respect the craft's transmission and the time needed to learn it. Sources disagree on some early chronology; avoid unsupported exact origin dates or a simplistic single-culture account of all marbling. Keep meaningful and sacred writing out of interchangeable decorative assets. A digital study should not claim practitioner endorsement, authentic physical production or completion of a craft apprenticeship. Use original application imagery and keep research reproductions linked unless reuse permission is explicit.

## Anti-Patterns

- Calling any fluid shader, oil-on-water photograph or psychedelic gradient Ebru.
- Generic smooth blobs with no nested pigment boundaries, tool rhythm or transfer premise.
- Treating all named branches as the same swirl effect or all marbling traditions as interchangeable.
- Copying recognizable practitioner sheets, mosque illustrations, signatures or sacred inscriptions.
- Dense multicolor texture under every form field and paragraph.
- Continuous motion with no way to hold, inspect or keep a still surface.
- Claiming a screen simulation teaches an authentic physical recipe or certifies skill.
