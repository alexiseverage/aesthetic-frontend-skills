# Die Neue Typographie

## Scope

Die Neue Typographie is interwar typographic reform: asymmetrical, standardized, sans-serif, photographic, and organized around information hierarchy rather than historical display convention. Use it for editorial, documentation, archive, dashboard, or signage surfaces where type structure is the primary visual system. It is not generic clean UI, Swiss corporate polish, or decorative Constructivist agitation.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: Black, white, and sparing red for hierarchy; gray and photo reproduction tones as support. Color remains subordinate to information order.

**Type**: Geometric sans-serif, lower-case preference, strong hierarchy, tabular alignment, standardized spacing, and type as information architecture. Serif, script, blackletter, and decorative display should not be the default voice.

**Texture**: Clean paper, photomechanical reproduction, thin rules, photo rectangles, and minimal surface interference. Texture should not distract from hierarchy.

**Shape**: Rectangular text blocks, rules, columns, bars, photo frames, and geometry in service of reading order. Diagonal accents may appear through Constructivist influence, but content structure leads.

**Motion**: Purposeful movement through hierarchy: scroll, reveal, or anchor jumps should clarify reading order. Avoid spectacle.

**Spatial**: Asymmetrical balance, generous margins, deliberate gutters, clear alignments, and photography placed as a structural element rather than wrapped decoration.

**Cultural markers**: Tschichold’s 1928 manifesto, rejection of centered axial composition, Bayer and Moholy-Nagy influences, sans-serif standardization, photo-type integration, and typography as functional discipline.

## Non-Negotiables

**Non-negotiables**: Hierarchy-first typography, asymmetrical grid logic, restrained palette, and sans-serif modernist discipline are mandatory. If decoration leads, the system has left this aesthetic.

## Connotation

The New Typography reads as efficient, modern, precise, reformist, and machine-age. Historically it belongs to interwar debates about standardization, modern life, and print reform, not to timeless corporate cleanliness.

## Related / Subsets

Related: `constructivism` supplies more political diagonal force; `ulm-school` extends comparable logic into postwar systems and signage; `swiss-international` later codifies disciplined typography at broad corporate scale. Subsets may emphasize manifesto-style print, catalog systems, or photo-led informational layouts.

## Frontend / UI Guidance

Let typography do the work: clear left edges, asymmetric content blocks, strong scale jumps, rules, captions, and photo/text modules. Use red only to establish reading order or actionable emphasis. Prefer predictable information architecture over decorative theming.

## CSS Translation

Use CSS grid or flex layouts with offset columns, strong margins, thin rules, high-contrast text, red accent tokens, and rectangular image crops. Avoid ornamental borders, textured backgrounds, centered brochure composition, heavy shadows, and decorative motion.

## Typography / Fonts

Use geometric or modernist sans families for headings and UI, with readable sans body text. Futura-like forms can work for display, but long text needs accessibility-minded spacing, size, and line height. Avoid blackletter, script, serif revival, and ornamental display as defaults.

## Cultural / Ethical Notes

Low identity risk, but historically precise. Do not erase the movement’s reform context or treat it as a universal “best typography” ideology. Keep accessibility and language support ahead of period purity.

## Anti-Patterns

Do not use centered wedding-invitation layouts, decorative flourishes, arbitrary red slashes, illegible all-lowercase gimmicks, stock-photo decoration, or Swiss-style corporate neutrality without the interwar reform logic.
