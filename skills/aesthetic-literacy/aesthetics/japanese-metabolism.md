# Japanese Metabolism

Postwar Japanese megastructure futurism: modular capsules, infrastructural frames, expandable cities, exposed service systems, and urban growth imagined as biological metabolism rather than fixed composition.

## Scope

Use this aesthetic only where its full visual system supports the product, editorial, cultural, or institutional context. Keep the translation anchored in the source profile instead of extracting isolated decorative motifs.

Origin context: Metabolism was launched by young Japanese architects around the 1960 World Design Conference, proposing cities and buildings that could grow, renew, and replace parts in response to postwar urbanization and technological optimism.

## 7-Dimension Profile

**Palette**: concrete gray, off-white capsules, black shadow lines, steel, traffic orange, blueprint cyan, sea/sky blue, and technical warning accents; palettes should feel infrastructural, not neon sci-fi.

**Type**: technical sans-serif labels, architectural drawing annotations, Japanese/English bilingual context where appropriate, numbered capsules, module IDs, and planning diagrams; avoid fake Japanese styling.

**Texture**: raw concrete, steel joints, glass, prefabricated panels, blueprint lines, aerial city maps, service pipes, and gridded megastructure frames.

**Shape**: capsules, plug-in modules, towers, branching frames, circular portholes, hex/cell diagrams, elevated decks, and repeated units attached to a larger infrastructural skeleton.

**Motion**: growth, docking, replacement, expansion, map zoom, phased construction, capsule insertion, and slow infrastructure pulses; avoid arcade spaceship effects.

**Spatial**: multi-level urban sections, megastructure grids, sectional drawings, clusters around cores, city-as-network layouts, and long-range planning timelines.

**Cultural markers**: Kiyonori Kikutake, Kisho Kurokawa, Fumihiko Maki, Kenzo Tange, 1960 World Design Conference, postwar reconstruction, Tokyo Bay proposals, Nakagin Capsule Tower, Expo 70.
